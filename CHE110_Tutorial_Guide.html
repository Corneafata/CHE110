Step-by-step recipe (use every time)

  1. Read the reaction first. Write it down and note each coefficient (e.g. 2A → 3B). You need them for the relation.
  2. Draw the graph of the species whose data are given: x = time, y = concentration, with units and a sensible scale. Make it large, on one graph paper, and freehand a smooth curve. If one or two points deviate (experimental error), don't force the curve through them; follow the overall trend.
  3. Average rate (two times given): read [conc] at exactly those two times from your graph (approximate values are fine). Compute d[A]/dt = (C₂ − C₁)/(t₂ − t₁), with units. Then average rate = (1/coefficient) × |d[A]/dt|.
  4. Instantaneous rate (one time given): mark that time on the curve, place the ruler so it touches the curve at that point and draw the tangent. Pick two points on the tangent line (reasonably far apart), read (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), and find slope = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). Then instantaneous rate = (1/coefficient) × |slope|.
  5. Show only what you used. Label the points you took from the graph (e.g. the two times and the tangent points). Don't label every point; it makes the graph messy.
  6. Units: concentration/time, for example mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹. Rates are always positive, so use the modulus (or a minus sign) for a reactant.
  7. Relations (if asked for another species): write the equal-sign relation with the coefficients, substitute the known rate, and solve (section 4).
Common errors the lecturer saw: using a different time interval from the one asked ("do it within that exact boundary"); forgetting the minus/modulus so the rate comes out negative; forgetting the 1/coefficient; not writing units; labelling every point; drawing four small graphs on one page.

1 · What is the rate of reaction?

SLIDES (L5–6)TUTORIAL
  • Chemical reaction: reactants transform into products with different composition and properties, by breaking and forming bonds. The products contain the same atoms as the reactants, rearranged. Signs: energy released (fizzing, burning), odour, change in temperature or pH, colour change, precipitate.
  • Rate of reaction = how fast the reaction occurs = the change in concentration of any reactant or product per unit time.
  • For A → B: the concentration of A decreases and that of B increases with time. Rate of disappearance of A = rate of appearance of B (when the coefficients are 1).
Rate = −Δ[A]/Δt = +Δ[B]/Δt

For the general reaction A + 2B → 3C + D (slide):

Rate = −d[A]/dt = −½ d[B]/dt = ⅓ d[C]/dt = d[D]/dt
  • How is rate measured? Concentration can't be measured directly, so we measure a property that is proportional to it: refractive index, absorbance, conductance, volume of gas, etc. Examples on the slide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (volume of O₂); CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH (conductance).
  • Units of rate: concentration ÷ time, e.g. mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (M s⁻¹) or mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹.
Sheet Q4 (written answer). Why can't the rate be found by dividing "the amount reacted" by the time taken? Because that gives only an average rate, not the actual rate at a moment. The rate isn't constant: it changes as the reactant concentration falls. The instantaneous rate comes from the slope of the tangent to the concentration–time curve at that moment.

2 · Reading the concentration–time graphs

TUTORIAL CLASSEXAM SIGNAL: explain / compare points

Reactant graph, [A] vs time (decreasing curve)

RegionWhat you seeWhy (the lecturer's reasoning)
StartSteepest slope, so the rate is fastestThe reactant concentration is highest, so there are more collisions and more effective collisions.
MiddleSlope gets gentler; the rate decreases gradually (not stopping)Reactant is being used up, so concentration falls, so effective collisions fall.
LateAlmost flat; concentration barely changesCalled equilibrium / completion in class: the change is so small you can't tell whether it is going on. Rate of forward and reverse change are equal (or the reaction is essentially complete). Changes are of the order of 0.05 or 0.01.
  • "Steeper = larger slope = higher rate." Exam-style comparison: "compare the rate at point P (early) with Q (late): P is higher because [A] is higher, so effective collisions are higher."
  • The lecturer noted "slowly" isn't quite the word for the middle region: the change still happens, just gradually.
  • In nature the reaction normally keeps changing very slowly; all reactions are reversible in principle but we don't give them enough time.
  • If you stop it (e.g. by removing product), the reaction ends.

Product graph, [B] vs time (increasing curve)

  • Starts at zero (there was no product initially), so the graph starts at the origin.
  • Steep at the start, then gradually flatter, then levels off (completion/equilibrium). Same reason: reactant is high initially, so effective collisions are high and product forms fastest at first.
  • The product's slope is positive, so no modulus is needed.
Drawing rules: x = time, y = concentration (don't plot values randomly). If you're asked for the product, the curve rises; for the reactant, it falls. An experimental point that goes off the trend (up then back down) is left off the curve.

3 · The three rates

TUTORIAL CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

You studied the individual rates (reactant's rate and product's rate). The overall rate of the reaction is looked at in three ways:

RateMeaningHow to find it
Initial rateRate at the very beginning (t = 0), before concentrations change muchTangent at t = 0. Not examined as a calculation here. Its use: for complex, multi-step reactions it helps predict how the reaction behaves and is used later for finding the order (initial-rate method), because later on you can't tell which reactant changed how much.
Average rateAverage change in concentration over a specific time intervalΔC/Δt between the two times given. Like average velocity = change in distance / change in time. Use exactly that interval, no more or less.
Instantaneous rateRate at one specific momentSlope of the tangent at that point on the curve (a single point has no "change", so we use the tangent / gradient).
Average rate = (1/coeff) · |[A]₂ − [A]₁| / (t₂ − t₁)
Instantaneous rate = (1/coeff) · |slope of tangent| , slope = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁)

The minus sign / modulus

  • Rates are always positive. For a reactant, d[A]/dt is negative, so put a minus sign in front or take the modulus |d[A]/dt|. The lecturer recommends the modulus: you'll never get the sign wrong.
  • For a product, d[B]/dt is already positive: no modulus needed.

Straight-line graph (MCQ signal)

If the concentration–time graph is a straight line (uniform change), then the slope is the same everywhere. So you don't need to draw a tangent, and the average rate equals the instantaneous rate at every time (any two points give the same ΔC/Δt). For a curved graph (the usual case) they differ.

4 · Coefficients and relating the rates

TUTORIAL CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

Why the coefficient appears

  • In 2A → B, A is consumed twice as fast as B forms. The reaction's rate is expressed "per single-fold", so you divide A's change by its coefficient 2 (multiply by ½). "Whatever coefficient is with whichever term, the coefficient goes to the other side in divided form."
  • If the coefficient is a fraction (½), you multiply by 2 instead.
  • This applies to any of the three rates (initial, average, instantaneous) when you express the overall rate through one species.

When do you use the coefficient and when not?

The question asks…Do this
"Rate of disappearance of A" or "rate of formation of B"Just |d[A]/dt| or d[B]/dt. No coefficient. It is that species' own rate.
"Average / instantaneous rate of the reaction" found from species AInclude 1/coefficient.
"Find the rate of B given the rate of A"Write the equal-sign relation with coefficients, substitute, solve.

The relation (for 2A → 3B)

½ |d[A]/dt| = ⅓ d[B]/dt

Both sides equal the same overall rate, whether average, instantaneous or initial.

For A + 2B → 3C + D

Rate = |d[A]/dt| = ½|d[B]/dt| = ⅓ d[C]/dt = d[D]/dt
  • Reactants are not given a plus sign: each graph is of one species individually (not a summation), so each term is separate and you just equate them.
  • Each species has its own graph and its own relation.
Class example. 2A → 3B. The rate of disappearance of A is 0.01 mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹. Rate of formation of B?
½(0.01) = ⅓ · d[B]/dt → d[B]/dt = 3 × 0.005 = 0.015 mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹. No graph needed: the rate is given, but you must use the reaction equation.

5 · Factors affecting the rate of reaction

TUTORIAL CLASSSLIDES

Theory only (no maths in the tutorial syllabus), but good for MCQ/short questions. Six factors on the slide: concentration, pressure (gases), temperature, catalyst, light, surface area.

FactorEffect and reason
ConcentrationHigher concentration → more collisions → more effective collisions → higher rate (rate ∝ cⁿ). Limit: if you keep increasing it until there is "no space left", it behaves like a solid, so space/surface area also matters.
Surface areaBigger surface area → faster reaction (reaction happens at the surface of a solid). A wood fire burns faster if logs are chopped into small pieces; a large ball broken into tiny particles has far more total surface. Nanomaterials and nanocatalysts use this. The rate rises with surface area per unit volume.
CatalystChanges the rate without being consumed. It provides a new route (pathway) with lower activation energy (Eₐ′); the original path is not changed or ruined. It takes part in at least one step and is regenerated. It can also add surface area. Examples: Ni, Fe, V₂O₅; most catalysts are transition metals (d-block). Analogy: choosing the shorter road to the same place.
LightPhotochemical reactions. Photons give the activation energy; e.g. Cl₂ + light → 2 Cl• (radicals, highly reactive, with an unpaired electron). Rate rises with light intensity (more photons). Example: photosynthesis.
PressureOnly for gases. Higher pressure/partial pressure → molecules closer → more collisions → more effective collisions. Little effect on liquids and solids (not compressible).
TemperatureRate usually increases with temperature. Higher temperature → higher kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) → more molecules with energy above the activation energy → more effective collisions.
Temperature, carefully (class): temperature does not lower the activation energy. The barrier stays the same; the reactant particles simply carry more energy (e.g. 10 J → 12 J versus a 10 J barrier) so they cross it more easily. Don't bring Le Chatelier's principle into this. Catalyst, by contrast, does lower Eₐ (by a new path).

Activation energy

  • Activation energy (Eₐ) = the minimum energy needed for the reactants to start the reaction (energy, not time). On a potential-energy vs reaction-progress diagram it's measured from the reactant level up to the peak (the barrier), not from zero.
  • Why a barrier? Bonds must break (A–A, B–B) and new bonds form (A–B). During collision, electron clouds repel each other, so potential energy rises first.
  • Spontaneous reaction: can cross the barrier on its own. Non-spontaneous: needs energy from outside (temperature, light) to make it go.
  • Lower potential energy = more stable. In the class diagram (an endothermic reaction) the product is higher in energy than the reactant, so the reactant is more stable.

Boltzmann / Maxwell distribution

  • Graph: number of molecules (y) vs kinetic energy (x). At a lower temperature most molecules have low energy. At a higher temperature the curve shifts right and flattens: more molecules have high energy.
  • So at higher T, a larger number of molecules exceed Eₐ (the threshold energy), giving more effective collisions.

Arrhenius equation (slide; calculations come later)

k = A · e^(−Eₐ/RT)    ln k = ln A − (Eₐ/R)(1/T)

k = rate constant; A = frequency factor; Eₐ = activation energy; R = gas constant (8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹); T = absolute temperature. Temperature coefficient = ratio of rate constants at two temperatures 10 °C apart.

Types of reactions (slides 3–9)

  • A chemical reaction rearranges atoms; products contain the same atoms as reactants.
  • Combustion (fire triangle: fuel, oxygen, heat). Acid-base: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CO₂ + H₂O + Na⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ (baking soda + vinegar releases CO₂). Precipitation: Ca²⁺ + C₂O₄²⁻ ⇌ CaC₂O₄(s) (kidney stones). Redox.

Rate law and order

rate = k [A]ᵐ [B]ⁿ
  • The rate is proportional to concentrations each raised to some power. The relationship found experimentally (by varying one concentration while keeping the other constant) is the rate law (rate equation). k is the rate constant.
  • Order of reaction = the sum of the powers in the rate law (overall order = m + n). Order with respect to A is m, with respect to B is n. The powers are not necessarily the coefficients; they are experimental.
  • Possible orders: 0, 1, 2, 3, 3/2, ½. Fractional (3/2, ½) only for gas-phase reactions. Third order is rare; most reactions are first order; zero and third order are rare.
  • Example: 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F, rate = k[NO₂][F₂] → first order in each, overall order 2.

Slide problems, solved

Zero order (slide 26). [A]₀ = 1.5 M, [A] = 0.75 M after 120 s. k = ([A]₀ − [A])/t = 0.75/120 = 6.25×10⁻³ M s⁻¹.
First order (slide 27). Unit of k: s⁻¹ (time⁻¹). A reaction is 35.5% complete in 4.90 min: [A]/[A]₀ = 0.645; k = (1/4.90) ln(1/0.645) = 0.4385/4.90 = 0.0895 min⁻¹.
Half-life, first order (slide 28). k = 0.450 s⁻¹ → t½ = 0.693/0.450 = 1.54 s. 50% complete in 23 min → k = 0.693/23 = 0.0301 min⁻¹; 90% complete: t = (1/k) ln(100/10) = 2.303/0.0301 = 76.4 min.
Second order (slide 29). Unit of k: M⁻¹ s⁻¹. k = 0.025 M⁻¹ s⁻¹, [A]₀ = 0.80 M, t = 200 s: 1/[A] = 1/0.80 + 0.025 × 200 = 1.25 + 5 = 6.25 → [A] = 0.16 M.
Half-life, second order (slide 30). t½ = 1/(k[A]₀) = 1/(0.010 × 0.50) = 200 s.

Finding order from initial-rate data (slides 33–35)

2NO + 2H₂ → N₂ + 2H₂O. Expt 1: [NO] 0.10, [H₂] 0.20, rate 2.4×10⁻³. Expt 2: 0.10, 0.10, 1.2×10⁻³. Expt 3: 0.20, 0.20, 9.6×10⁻³.
1 vs 2: [NO] fixed, [H₂] halved → rate halves → first order in H₂. 1 vs 3: [H₂] fixed, [NO] doubled → rate ×4 → second order in NO.
Rate = k[NO]²[H₂]; overall order 3. k = 2.4×10⁻³ /(0.10² × 0.20) = 1.2 M⁻² s⁻¹.

SO₂Cl₂ → SO₂ + Cl₂. Rate is 3.60×10⁻⁶ M s⁻¹ at 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75 M: no change with concentration → zero order; k = 3.60×10⁻⁶ M s⁻¹.

Decomposition of O₃. [O₃] 0.020, 0.040, 0.060 → rate 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 ×10⁻⁵. Rate ∝ [O₃] → first order; k = 1.0×10⁻⁵/0.020 = 5.0×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
Method: compare two experiments in which only one concentration changes. If doubling it doubles the rate → order 1; quadruples → order 2; no change → order 0.

Numerical drill: average rate

Cheat sheet

Graph: x = time, y = concentration; large; freehand smooth; ignore outliers; label only used points.
Reactant falls, steepest at start (high [A] → more effective collisions). Product starts at 0 and rises, then levels.
Average rate = (1/coeff)·|ΔC/Δt| over the exact two times given.
Instantaneous = (1/coeff)·|slope of tangent|; slope = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) from two points on the tangent.
Initial rate: t = 0 (used for order).
Straight line: avg = instantaneous.
Units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (or min⁻¹).
"Rate of disappearance of A" = |d[A]/dt| (no coefficient). Reaction rate / relation: divide by coefficient.
aA+bB→cC+dD: rate = |d[A]/dt|/a = |d[B]/dt|/b = d[C]/dt/c = d[D]/dt/d.
Factors: concentration, pressure (gases), temperature, catalyst, light, surface area.
Catalyst lowers Eₐ via a new path. Temperature doesn't lower Eₐ; more molecules exceed it.
Bring: calculator, pencil, scale, 2 graph papers.
