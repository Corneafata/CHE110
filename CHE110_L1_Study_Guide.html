  9. Do not memorise masses, charges and mass numbers of electron, proton and neutron.

1 · The nucleus and its forces

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

Picture of the atom

  • Neutrons and protons sit in the middle (the nucleus); electrons are around it.
  • Space: most of the atom is empty. The nucleus takes up very little space; the electrons take up most of it. (That is what Rutherford found.)
  • Mass: the nucleus is far heavier than the electrons.

Which force holds what?

What is heldForce
Neutrons and protons in the nucleusStrong nuclear force
Electrons and the nucleusCoulombic (electrostatic) attraction between negative and positive
The lecturer's words: "In every semester a small question like this is given. With the help of which force do the neutron and proton stay together in the nucleus? … Strong nuclear force. Look, the force's name itself is nuclear force." A common wrong answer is magnetic force. Remember: electron–nucleus = Coulombic; protons and neutrons = strong nuclear force.

Why is a strong force needed?

  • Protons carry positive charge. There is no negative charge inside the nucleus, so everything there is positive. Same charges repel.
  • Many protons packed into a tiny space should fly apart. They don't, because the strong nuclear force binds them. The lecturer compared it to glue (gluon-like) that binds neutron and proton.
  • The strong force works only over very short distances (a space too small to see). If you increase the distance between the particles, the strong force can no longer hold them.
  • The nucleus does not break easily; ordinary atoms (H, N, O) stay intact. It breaks only if it is radioactive or hit by outside radiation. Very high atomic number → radioactive (a later topic).
  • The proton number defines the atom. If the proton number changed, the atom would become a different element, so the atom does not let protons leave easily.

What are neutrons and protons made of?

Quarks (charged, positive and negative). For the neutron, the quark charges add and subtract to zero, so it is "zero charge". For the proton, they add up to +1.

Role of the neutron: the "peacemaker"

  • Neutrons sit between protons and create a little distance, so repulsion is reduced.
  • n : p ratio of 1 : 1 → stable. Example: carbon-12 (6 p, 6 n).
  • Too many neutrons ("too many peacemakers become a problem") → the nucleus is unstable and radioactive. Example: carbon-14 (6 p, 8 n), used in carbon dating.
  • Slight changes are fine: sodium has 11 p and 12 n. A ratio roughly between 1 and 1.5 causes no problem. Beyond that, the nucleus becomes radioactive.
  • Excess protons or excess neutrons both lead to radioactivity ("if it is not balanced, it will be unstable").
stable: n ≈ p (1 : 1 up to ~1.5 : 1)  |  unbalanced → radioactive

Notation

  • An atom is written with its mass number (top left) and atomic/proton number (bottom left).
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = mass number − atomic number.

Electron mass

The electron's mass is about 10⁻³¹ kg. It is negligible compared with the neutron and proton, but negligible is not the same as zero. It has mass and it is countable. (You do not need to memorise the exact values.)

2 · Rutherford's model

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

What Rutherford did and said

  • Discovered the nucleus: a positively charged centre. He is "the father of the nucleus".
  • He did not determine the proton and neutron numbers (how many of each).
  • He compared the atom to the solar system: electrons revolve like planets.
  • He said something about electron motion but gave no mathematical explanation.
  • Gold-foil experiment: he passed alpha particles through the atom (foil). Most went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space. (Full result in the recap, section 10.)

The problem with Rutherford's model

Lecturer's argument. An electron keeps revolving, so it has velocity. A moving body eventually loses energy. Planets are neutral. An electron is a charged particle moving around an opposite charge (the nucleus), so as it loses energy it comes closer to the nucleus and finally falls in. Then the atom would no longer exist.

Maxwell said exactly this: a moving charged body loses energy and will fall. Rutherford implied it won't fall. Their statements are contradictory, and Maxwell is correct here.
True/false signal: "Rutherford's and Maxwell's statements are opposite to each other" is TRUE. The lecturer noted that some students wrongly marked it false.
  • The analogy itself is weak: planets are neutral, electrons are charged. "Their way and these guys' way will not be the same. Never will be."
  • Many other things were not explained properly by the model.
  • Its good aspect: the nucleus carries positive charge, and the atom is mostly empty space.

3 · Bohr's model

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

Bohr "updated" Rutherford: the positive nucleus is fine, but the electron does not move randomly.

  • Each electron has a specific place, called an orbit. The concept of orbit came first from Bohr's model. Orbits are circular (circumference 2πr).
  • Hydrogen has one orbit occupied (one electron). Atoms beyond H and He have more orbits.
  • In a specific orbit, the electron has a fixed velocity and fixed energy. The word for fixed is quantized (a discrete level of energy).
  • Every electron in the first orbit has the same energy; the second orbit has a different, fixed energy; the third has another. Each orbit carries a fixed energy and never a random one.
  • Orbits are also called shells or energy shells.

Electron: wave and particle

The electron has mass (particle nature) and revolves like a wave (wave nature). So it has a dual nature, like light. It is never 50/50: if one nature is very high, the other is negligible. (This comes from de Broglie's theory, taught later.)

Angular momentum is quantized EXAM SIGNAL

m v r = n h / 2π    n = 1, 2, 3, 4, …
  • The electron has mass m, velocity v and radius r from the centre, so it has angular momentum (mvr).
  • Bohr said angular momentum increases in steps of h/2π. n says how many times: once, twice, three times… so n is a natural number (positive integer starting from 1).
  • MCQ / short-question signal: "Which of the following did Bohr explain?" Answer: angular momentum is quantized (with respect to h/2π). He did not say "energy is quantized" directly; that came later. Quantizing one thing implies the rest (energy, velocity) get quantized.
  • Bohr gave formulas but couldn't explain beyond hydrogen: for electrons in the 2nd, 3rd orbit he couldn't provide full mathematical expressions for energy and velocity.
n is the ORBIT number, not the orbital. The values of n always denote the orbit number or energy-shell number. An orbital is a specific place that stays within an orbit (taught later). The lecturer: "I am not teaching orbital, I am teaching orbit."

Planck's constant, h

  • h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (given in the exam).
  • Role: a parameter to compare the classical world with the quantum world. Whenever you study anything about electrons in atoms, h appears, and everything is compared with it as the standard. It introduces you to the quantum world.

Why Bohr's model beats Rutherford's

It explains the electron's place mathematically: the place is not random, and a specific place means a fixed velocity and fixed energy.

"Semi-classical" model. Bohr said angular momentum is quantized; the phrase "energy is quantized" came much later. His model is semi-classical: he couldn't fully move to quantum theory nor break out of classical theory. But the concept of quantum theory started from him. "Quantized" is only possible for tiny invisible objects like atoms; our everyday energy does not become quantized. (His full postulates and limitations are in the slides; a long written question on them is not expected.)

4 · Energy levels, packets and excitation

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

The lecturer's example. Level 1 has energy 20 J and level 2 has 50 J. To go from level 1 to level 2 you need 30 J. Photon energy packets of 10, 20, 25, 30 and 40 J are sent. Which does the electron absorb? Try it:

Send a packet of:
Level 1 = 20 J, level 2 = 50 J. Needed = 30 J. Only the 30 J packet is absorbed.
  • Only 30 J is absorbed. The electron never absorbs random energy. It receives exactly the amount that pushes it to the higher level in one go.
  • You cannot make 30 J by combining 10 J + 20 J: it won't "sit taking 10 J, waiting for 20 J". Packets are not added up.
  • These energy packets are photons.
  • Quantized energy = fixed energy carried, and fixed energy received.

Empty spaces between levels, and nodes

  • Like stairs: you stand on one step or another, never in the gap between. You cannot climb halfway.
  • Electrons are never found in these in-between spaces. The electron exists as a cloud with non-uniform density. At the edge of the cloud the probability of finding the electron is very low, essentially zero. Such places are called nodes.
  • The lecturer said questions will come from nodes, but nodes will be explained properly in a later class. For now: node = place where the probability of finding an electron is (close to) zero.

Excited state and coming back

  • An electron that receives the right energy goes to a higher level: the excited level.
  • Nobody stays excited for long, so the electron comes back to a lower level. It must release the extra energy: from 50 J to 20 J it releases 30 J.
  • For hydrogen's single electron, only the first shell is in use normally; the other shells exist but matter only when the electron is excited.

5 · Emission and ΔE = hc/λ

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hc / λ
  • h = Planck's constant; c = speed of light; λ = wavelength of the radiation.
  • This gives the energy difference. (The lecturer noted this does not itself explain quantization; the formula for quantized energy is different.)
  • Equivalent form: ΔE = hc · (1/λ). If you already have 1/λ from the Rydberg formula, multiply by hc to get ΔE directly.

Why we measure differences, and why emission

  • We always talk in differences: temperature "15° higher", "2° higher than yesterday". Energy is the same: compare before and after.
  • The absorbed energy can't be seen or directly measured. Analogy: a sponge soaks up water. You can only measure how much water it held when you squeeze it out (release).
  • What we measure is the emitted energy (high → low). That is why we see electromagnetic radiation: these are all emission rays.
  • The emitted radiation can be anywhere in the electromagnetic spectrum: radio, microwave, infrared, UV, visible. If it is visible, we see it.
  • Absorbed wavelength can be found with a separate instrument, but the standard formulas in this course are for emitted light.

6 · The Rydberg formula

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL: maths in mid, quiz and final
1/λ = RH ( 1/n₁² − 1/n₂² )
  • RH = Rydberg constant = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ (given in the exam, with units).
  • n₁ = lower level (final level). n₂ = higher level (initial level).
  • Don't forget the squares! They came from the derivation; you don't need the derivation, but you do need the squares.
  • The formula gives the λ of emitted light.
Why n₂ (the higher level) is "initial". The transition (emission) starts at the higher level and ends at the lower. So initial = higher = n₂, final = lower = n₁. "Find the initial level" means find n₂.
The 1 and 2 are only serial labels: you can write them as n_lower / n_higher or n_final / n_initial. The lecturer said the naming is up to you; what matters is placing the values correctly.
Why the larger n goes in the second term. The start is the larger value, and mathematically the larger value has to be kept later, otherwise the result becomes negative. If your 1/λ comes out negative, you swapped n₁ and n₂.

Reading a transition

"The transition is 4 to 1" means the electron goes from n = 4 to n = 1. The first number is the initial (higher), the second is the final (lower).

Z and hydrogen

The general formula has a Z (atomic number). For hydrogen Z = 1, so it is not needed. The lecturer: questions are always set for hydrogen, so ignore Z.

Procedure (use every time)

  1. Identify n₂ (initial, higher) and n₁ (final, lower).
  2. Compute 1/λ = RH(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²). (1/∞ = 0.)
  3. Invert: λ = 1/(1/λ). Unit: m.
  4. Energy: ΔE = hc × (1/λ) in J.
  5. Write the unit with the answer.
Common slips: forgetting the square · swapping n₁/n₂ (negative answer) · giving 1/λ instead of λ · forgetting the unit.

The lecturer's practice problem

The transition is from n = ∞ to n = 1. Find λ and ΔE. (RH = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, c = 3×10⁸ m/s, h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s.)
1/λ = RH(1/1² − 1/∞²) = RH(1 − 0) = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ → λ = 9.1×10⁻⁸ m
ΔE = hc/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(3×10⁸)(1.097×10⁷) = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ J
"1/infinity is zero." No unit conversion needed: leave λ in m.

7 · Energy rises with n; energy and wavelength

TAUGHT IN CLASS

Why does a farther level have more energy?

  • Levels farther from the nucleus have higher energy values: E₁ < E₂ < E₃ … and n = ∞ has the most.
  • The lecturer's reasoning (not about attraction!): to keep an electron far away, it has to be carrying more energy, "otherwise it won't be able to stay". The value of that level itself is higher. As n increases, velocity and radius also change, and the energy value rises.
  • Don't confuse the two ideas: a far level has a higher energy but a weaker attraction to the nucleus. Higher energy does not mean higher attraction.
  • It is easier to ionize an electron in a far level (weaker attraction), even though the level's energy is higher.
  • No electrons have been seen beyond about the 7th shell for the 118 known elements.
Follow-up in Lecture 2. At the start of Lecture 2 the lecturer, answering a student, said the first-orbit electron has the "highest" energy and that energy "decreases" going higher. She was talking about binding energy (how strongly the electron is held / the energy needed to free it), which is largest at n = 1 and falls with n. The level energy E = −13.6 eV/n² still rises toward 0 as n grows. If a question says "ionization / energy needed to remove", it falls with n; if it says "energy of the orbit/level", it rises with n. Details in the Lecture 2 guide.

Energy and wavelength are inversely related

From ΔE = hc/λ:

  • Large ΔE → small λ (higher frequency, more vibration).
  • Small ΔE → large λ (low frequency, slow waves).
  • Biggest gap: n = ∞ to n = 1 → smallest λ. With n₁ fixed, n₂ = ∞ gives the smallest λ.
  • Wavelength = distance from one crest to the next (or trough to trough). Lower wavelength → higher energy → higher frequency.

8 · Spectrum

TAUGHT IN CLASSMCQ / TRUE-FALSE SIGNAL
  • White light through a prism gives seven colours: VIBGYOR (violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red). All seven are inside white light. These sit in the visible range of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • Besides visible, radiation includes radio, microwave, infrared, UV and more; each has its own wavelength.
  • A spectrum is understood in a broad sense, but also by specific lines.

  • All the hydrogen series (Lyman, Balmer…) are emission series, so the transition is high → low.

9 · The five series

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL: know the order
Know the sequence (the question will NOT give you n₁): Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund = lower limit n₁ = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.
Mnemonic: Long Before People Became Punctual.
SeriesLower (final) level n₁Electrons come from n₂ =
Lyman12, 3, 4, … ∞
Balmer23, 4, 5, … ∞
Paschen34, 5, 6, … ∞
Brackett45, 6, … ∞
Pfund56, 7, … ∞

Why does a series have a lower limit?

In the lecturer's words: an electron sent to a high level can fall to any lower level (10 → 9, 9 → 7, 7 → 2…), which is random. Balmer instead sent electrons up and fixed the lower level at 2, so every transition from a higher level ends at 2 (3→2, 4→2, … ∞→2). Paschen fixed level 3; Lyman fixed level 1. Each series has a lower limit and "didn't work randomly". Each transition releases energy, so each fixed level gives a spectrum series.

Maximum and minimum wavelength (idea taught here; the maths was done in Lecture 2)

  • Within a series, the transition from the closest level (3→2 in Balmer; 2→1 in Lyman) has the smallest energy difference → maximum λ.
  • The transition from infinity has the largest energy difference → minimum λ.

Class question: find the initial level

The wavelength of a line in the Balmer series is 6.56×10⁻⁷ m. Find the initial energy level.
The initial level is n₂ (the higher). Balmer → n₁ = 2.
1/λ = 1/(6.56×10⁻⁷) = 1.524×10⁶ m⁻¹
1.524×10⁶ = 1.097×10⁷ (1/4 − 1/n₂²) → 1/4 − 1/n₂² = 0.1389 → 1/n₂² = 0.1111 → n₂ = 3. The transition is 3 → 2.

The lecturer said you don't need to memorise the spectral region of each series. (In passing: Lyman is in the ultraviolet, Balmer in the visible, the rest infrared. The lecturer misspoke "infrared" for Lyman in class; it is not needed for the exam.)

10 · End-of-class recap (from the slides)

TAUGHT IN CLASS
  • Two facts to remember: electrons and nucleus are attracted by Coulombic attraction; protons and neutrons stay together by the strong nuclear force.
  • Gold foil (slide picture): the green balls are alpha particles. Three conclusions on the slide: most α pass through undeflected; some scatter at large angles; a very small number rebound by 180°. The nucleus occupies a tiny space and is positive, and α is also positive, so like charges repel and the α goes back. Only 2–3 in a hundred or two hundred rebound in the picture. (The lecturer also described the deflected ones as being affected by the charges in the atom; the key idea is that α particles are positive.)
  • Electron stays in place because the inward pull (the centre attracts) and the outward force from its motion balance each other.
  • Ionization: to remove an electron you must give energy from outside, and something must also be ready to accept it. Ionization energy is endothermic. The orbit has a specific energy, so you must supply energy from outside before the electron leaves. Example: sodium gives an electron and becomes stable (octet) only because another atom takes it.
  • Bohr: angular momentum quantized; "energy quantized" came much later; semi-classical model; quantum theory started from him.
  • Spectrum: line = specific wavelengths, separate. Continuous = all wavelengths together (red to violet). White light is continuous.
  • The lecturer joked that chemistry has exceptions (Kota Factory), because invisible things can behave differently in different situations.

Worked problems

Constants (given in the exam): RH = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹, h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3×10⁸ m/s. Answers in metres and joules; no conversion needed.

1. (Class) ∞ → 1. 1/λ = RH(1 − 0) → λ = 9.1×10⁻⁸ m; ΔE = hc/λ = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ J.
2. (Class) Balmer line, λ = 6.56×10⁻⁷ m. Find the initial level. n₂ = 3 (see section 9).
3. 3 → 2. 1/λ = R(1/4 − 1/9) = 5R/36 = 1.524×10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 6.56×10⁻⁷ m. ΔE = hc/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(3×10⁸)/(6.56×10⁻⁷) = 3.03×10⁻¹⁹ J.
4. 4 → 2 (Balmer). 1/λ = R(1/4 − 1/16) = 3R/16 = 2.057×10⁶ m⁻¹ → λ = 4.86×10⁻⁷ m. ΔE = 4.09×10⁻¹⁹ J.
5. 4 → 1 (Lyman). 1/λ = R(1 − 1/16) = 15R/16 = 1.028×10⁷ m⁻¹ → λ = 9.72×10⁻⁸ m. ΔE = 2.04×10⁻¹⁸ J.
6. 5 → 3 (Paschen). 1/λ = R(1/9 − 1/25) = 16R/225 = 7.80×10⁵ m⁻¹ → λ = 1.282×10⁻⁶ m. ΔE = 1.55×10⁻¹⁹ J.
7. Smallest λ in Balmer (n₂ = ∞): 1/λ = R/4 → λ = 4/R = 3.65×10⁻⁷ m. Largest λ in Lyman (2 → 1): 1/λ = 3R/4 → λ = 4/(3R) = 1.215×10⁻⁷ m. Done on the board in Lecture 2, where it was called a common exam question. See the full table for all five series.
8. Packet problem (class). E₁ = 20 J, E₂ = 50 J → need 30 J. Only the 30 J packet is absorbed. On de-excitation 50 → 20 releases 30 J.
Check your answer: bigger gap → smaller λ. ∞→1 gives about 9.1×10⁻⁸ m, the shortest for hydrogen. If your λ for 3→2 isn't about 6.56×10⁻⁷ m, recheck.

Matter and atoms

  • Matter = anything with mass that occupies space. It is a pure substance (element or compound) or a mixture.
  • Element: made of only one kind of atom. Compound: two or more kinds of atoms joined in a definite proportion.
  • Atom: extremely small particle of matter that retains its identity during chemical reactions. Everything (a table, a chair, you) is made of atoms.
  • Particle data (slide; the lecturer said don't memorise the numbers): electron 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C (negative), 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg (>1800× lighter than hydrogen); proton +1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, 1.672×10⁻²⁷ kg; neutron 0 C, 1.674×10⁻²⁷ kg. The nucleus holds protons and neutrons (except hydrogen: one proton).
  • Atoms have size of the order of 10⁻¹⁰ m. Protons and neutrons make up the nucleus and nearly all the mass; electrons take up almost all the volume.

Dalton's atomic theory (1766–1844)

Democritus first suggested the atom; Dalton placed it on a solid footing about two millennia later. Based on two laws:

  • Law of conservation of mass: matter is neither created nor destroyed in a closed system (why we balance equations). Example: 10 g of wood burned in a sealed container gives 10 g of smoke, ash and gas.
  • Law of constant composition: a pure compound always has the same proportion of the same elements. NaCl is always the same Na : Cl ratio; H₂O is always 2 H : 1 O, whether tap or river water.
  1. All matter is composed of minute particles called atoms; atoms are indivisible and indestructible and retain identity in a reaction.
  2. All atoms of a given element have the same properties, including mass.
  3. Atoms of different elements have different properties and masses.
  4. A compound is atoms of two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions (water: H : O = 2 : 1).
  5. A chemical reaction is the rearrangement of atoms into new combinations. Only atoms take part.
  6. Atoms are not created, destroyed or broken into smaller particles in a reaction.

Limitations of Dalton's theory

Dalton saidWhy it fails
Atoms are indivisibleAtoms have protons, neutrons and electrons.
Atoms of the same element have the same massIsotopes: hydrogen has masses 1, 2 and 3.
Atoms of different elements have different massesIsobars: Te (Z = 52) and I (Z = 53) both have mass 127.
Atom is the smallest part of both element and compoundThe atom is the smallest part of an element that can exist freely; the molecule is the smallest part of a compound. Dalton didn't distinguish them.

Thomson's model (1897) and the electron

  • Cathode-ray tube: two electrodes (negative cathode, positive anode) sealed in a glass tube with the air removed, connected to a high-voltage supply. The glass glows green. Rays leave the cathode, and the beam bends away from the negative plate and toward the positive plate, so cathode rays are negatively charged particles (electrons), a constituent of all matter.
  • Atoms are neutral, so Thomson proposed the plum-pudding model: negative electrons embedded in a diffuse sphere of positive charge. Postulate 1: positively charged sphere with electrons embedded. Postulate 2: atom is neutral because + and − charges are equal in magnitude.
  • Limitations: could not explain how the positive charge holds the electrons or the stability of the atom; no nucleus; couldn't explain Rutherford's scattering experiment.

Rutherford: extra slide details

  • 1911, gold foil (α-scattering). α particles come from a radioactive source at very high speed (about 180,000 miles per second on the slide).
  • Postulates (slide): most of an atom is empty; most of the mass (99.95% or more) is in the tiny, positive nucleus; electrons move around the nucleus like planets (solar-system / planetary / nuclear model); number of electrons = positive charge of the nucleus so the atom is neutral; the inward electrostatic (centripetal) force is exactly balanced by the outward centrifugal force.
  • Nuclei have diameters about 10⁻¹⁵ m; atoms about 10⁻¹⁰ m (so the atom is ~10⁵ times wider).
  • Limitations (slide): (1) By Maxwell, a rotating charged body must radiate energy continuously, so the electron would spiral into the nucleus. (2) Newton's laws apply to neutral bodies such as planets, not charged electrons; electrons repel one another while planets attract. (3) No idea about the shape of the orbits. (4) No explanation for many-electron atoms. (5) Continuous radiation would give a continuous (band) spectrum, but atoms give a line spectrum.

Bohr: extra slide details

  • 1913. The first model with quantum energy levels; Nobel Prize in 1922.
  • Postulates (slide): (1) Electrons revolve in special discrete orbits without radiating energy. (2) Each orbit is a shell / energy level with a specific amount of energy. (3) Absorbing energy to move from a lower to a higher level gives an excited state. (4) Radiating energy when moving from higher to lower. (5) Moving between orbits emits photons, producing characteristic absorption and emission spectra; since every element has its own signature, spectra identify materials. (6) Closer orbits (lower energy) are more stable; the lowest-energy orbit is the ground state.
  • Quantization of angular momentum: mvr = nh/2π. Energy radiated when an electron goes from E₂ to E₁ is E₂ − E₁ = hν.
  • Limitations (slide): only single-electron species (hydrogen-like); cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines (implies sub-levels → new quantum numbers); cannot explain splitting of lines in magnetic or electric fields; electrons jumping between orbits isn't justified because of uncertainty in position and velocity; circular instead of elliptical path.

Spectrum definitions (slides)

  • White light through a prism resolves into colours of different wavelengths. Newton (17th century) showed sunlight contains many colours.
  • Continuous spectrum: contains all wavelengths (rainbow). Line spectrum: only certain colours / specific wavelengths.
  • Slide Q: is an incandescent bulb's spectrum continuous or line? Continuous: it is like white light and disperses into all wavelengths without gaps.
  • Emission spectrum: heated atoms emit light of certain wavelengths: bright lines (electrons jumping from excited to ground state). Each element has a unique bright-line spectrum.
  • Absorption spectrum: white light through a substance leaves dark lines where wavelengths were absorbed.
