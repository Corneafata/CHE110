CHE110 · Lecture 7: Mechanism, energy diagrams, Arrhenius

Built from the transcript "L6" (it covers Lecture 7 on your slides) and the Lecture 7 slides. TAUGHT IN CLASS EXAM SIGNAL SLIDES

Midterm: the syllabus runs through Lecture 8, so all of this page is in scope. Format (20 marks, 1 hour) and strategy: Midterm prep.

What the lecturer said about the exam

  1. Elementary vs composite: a question tells you whether a reaction is elementary ("don't panic about these"; you won't be asked to memorise which reactions are elementary). If it is elementary, write the rate law with the coefficients as powers. If not stated, use x and y.
  2. Short 1–1.5 mark questions: "Why are high-molecularity reactions rare?" and "Write two differences between molecularity and order."
  3. MCQs: "What is the molecularity / overall order of this elementary reaction?" (options 0, 1, 2, 3) and the true/false "In any reaction the slowest step is the rate-determining step" (True: many wrongly mark it false).
  4. Potential-energy diagram questions are in the exam, in "creative" style: a stem, then draw the graph (2 marks), indicate transition state, activated complex, reactant, product, activation energy and ΔH; state exo/endo; find ΔH or find the activation energy from given energies. "Small things, not heavy maths." The slides' labelling is not very good: follow the class.
  5. Mark the activation energy from the reactant level, not from the axis.
  6. Activation energy does NOT decrease when you raise the temperature. ("This is what you guys write.") Reactants gain kinetic energy and cross the barrier faster.
  7. Arrhenius: 1–2 mark questions: slope of ln k vs 1/T gives −Ea/R; the two-temperature formula; MCQs. Derivations are not given but you should know how to derive so the formula doesn't go wrong.
  8. Maths pitfalls: temperature in Kelvin (+273); Ea in J/mol (convert kJ by × 1000, since R is in J); remove ln first (use e^) before moving numbers; write units. Practice questions are at the end of the practice-problem PDF (answers may have typos; follow the method).
  9. Penalties noted for the last tutorial: missing units, wrong calculator entries, not dividing by the coefficient, tangents that pass through two points, extra writing.

1 · Elementary and composite reactions

CLASSSLIDES 3–5
  • All reaction types (excluding radioactive/nuclear) are elementary or composite.
  • Elementary reaction = a single-step reaction (single collision): reactants directly convert to products in one molecular event. No hidden steps, no intermediates. Example: H₂ + I₂ → 2HI (one H₂ and one I₂ collide and give two HI: 5 H₂ + 5 I₂ gives 10 HI).
  • Composite (complex) reaction = multi-step: several elementary reactions summed up, with intermediates and transition states before the final products. Real-life reactions are mostly composite (water splitting into H₂ and O₂ is not one collision). "Balancing with 10 or 20 coefficients" gives composite reactions.
  • Elementary reactions are rare in real life (excluding radioactive ones). A single step doesn't mean slow: more steps with intermediates take longer.
  • Why it matters: for an elementary reaction you can tell the order by looking at the coefficients, which you cannot do for a composite one.

2 · Molecularity

CLASSSLIDES 3–6EXAM SIGNAL
  • Molecularity = the number of reactant particles (molecules or atoms) taking part in an elementary reaction. Products don't count.
MolecularityNameGeneral formExamples (slide)
1UnimolecularA → productsBr₂ → 2Br; CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g); cyclopropane → propene
2BimolecularA + B → products; A + A → products2HI → H₂ + I₂; CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH; 2NOCl → 2NO + Cl₂; H₂ + I₂ → 2HI
3TermolecularA + B + C → products (A + 2B counts as 3)2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂; 2NO + Cl₂ → 2NOCl
  • In class: "A → products" has molecularity 1; "A + 2B → products" has 3 particles → termolecular. For H₂ + I₂ → 2HI: molecularity 2 (bimolecular).
  • Why are high-molecularity reactions rare? (1–1.5 marks) The reaction needs a simultaneous collision of the reacting particles with enough energy and correct orientation. As the number of particles increases, the chance of all colliding together effectively falls, so reactions of molecularity 4 or more essentially don't happen; molecularity 2 is the commonest, 3 is rare, 1 is easy (a particle breaks on its own).
  • Molecularity is a positive whole number: never 0 and never a fraction (a reactant doesn't "come with its head but not its legs").
  • Molecularity belongs only to elementary reactions (or to each elementary step of a mechanism). It is meaningless for an overall composite reaction (at best it is the molecularity of the rate-determining step; the book example of N₂O₅ gives 2).

3 · Order vs molecularity

CLASSSLIDE 61-MARK QUESTION: two differences
OrderMolecularity
Sum of the powers of the concentration terms in the rate lawNumber of reacting species undergoing simultaneous collision in an elementary reaction
Experimentally determinedTheoretical (read from the elementary reaction)
Can be fractionalAlways a whole number
Can be zeroCannot be zero
Can change with conditions (pressure, temperature, concentration)Invariant for a given chemical equation
Why is the molecularity of an elementary step equal to its order? Rate ∝ number of collisions ∝ concentration of each colliding species. For 2A + B → products: rate ∝ [A][A][B], so rate = k[A]²[B]: order 3 = molecularity 3. For H₂ + I₂ → 2HI: rate = k[H₂][I₂], order 2 = molecularity 2.
  • Not equal for composite reactions: the powers (orders) on the slides were found by experiment ("don't memorise them; they did experiments"). If a question says "elementary", use the coefficients; if not, write x and y and the overall order as x + y.
  • MCQ style: "Given this elementary reaction, what is its molecularity / overall order?" Just add the coefficients of the reactants.

4 · Reaction mechanism, rate-determining step, intermediates

CLASSSLIDES 7, 9TRUE/FALSE SIGNAL
  • A reaction mechanism is a step-by-step description of how a reaction occurs at the molecular level: the sequence of elementary steps, the formation and consumption of intermediates, and the role of catalysts.
  • Some steps are fast, others slow. The slowest step is the rate-determining step (RDS). It controls the overall rate. Analogy: a three-step reaction taking two hours where one step takes one and a half hours; or the traffic signal where you lose most of your journey time.
  • Finding the mechanism of every step is impractical, so we study the slowest step and write the rate law with respect to it.
  • True/false: "In any reaction the slowest step is the rate-determining step." → True. If two steps are both slow, the one that is the slowest is the RDS.
  • Speeding up a reaction: apply the factors (catalyst, surface area, temperature, light, pressure for gases) to the slowest step. Example: Raney nickel in hydrogenation (alkyne → alkene): the H–H bond (≈ 435 kJ/mol) is strong, so breaking it is slow; H₂ adsorbs on the nickel surface, the H–H bond weakens and H atoms are added to carbon. So the catalyst helps the slow step.

Slide example: decomposition of N₂O₅

Overall: 2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂ (a composite reaction, 3 steps):
Step 1: 2N₂O₅ → 2NO₂ + 2NO₃  (slow)
Step 2: NO₂ + NO₃ → NO + NO₂ + O₂  (slow, the slowest: rate-determining step)
Step 3: NO + NO₃ → 2NO₂  (fast)
Intermediates: NO₃ and NO (formed in one step, used up in another; they don't appear in the net equation). Because intermediates matter, you can't write the order from the overall coefficients.
  • Reaction intermediate: a species produced during an elementary step but not in the net reaction equation (NO₃ in N₂O₅ decomposition).

6 · Potential-energy diagrams

CLASSSLIDES 9–12EXAM SIGNAL: draw, label, calculate
  • Lower potential energy = more stable (more stable the lower and the more negative). High potential energy = unstable and restless.
  • The diagram plots potential energy (y) against reaction progress (x). For an elementary reaction there is one hump (one Ea); for a multi-step reaction there are several humps, one activation energy for each step.
  • Every reaction must be spontaneous to proceed: exothermic reactions are spontaneous (no outside energy needed); endothermic reactions need energy from outside (temperature, light) to start and keep going.

What happens along the curve (class example: AB + C → A + BC)

  1. Reactant at some energy (10 J in the class example).
  2. When the reactants collide, their electron clouds repel and the bond in AB must start to break, so potential energy rises (rearrangement; this energy is taken from the system's stored energy).
  3. At the top is the transition state (50 J): bond breaking and forming at once. The grouping of atoms there (A···B···C) is the activated complex: unstable, momentary, highest energy. Slide: it has higher PE than reactants or products and decomposes at a definite rate to give products.
  4. Then bond formation releases energy and the energy falls to the product.
TermMeaning / how to find
Activation energy, EₐMinimum energy needed to start the reaction = E(transition state) − E(reactant). Marked from the reactant level up to the peak, not from the axis ("I don't have any particle at that level").
ΔHE(product) − E(reactant).
ExothermicProduct lower than reactant → ΔH negative; product is the most stable; energy released; spontaneous; no external energy.
EndothermicProduct higher than reactant → ΔH positive; the reactant is more stable; energy absorbed from the surroundings.
Class numbers. Exothermic: reactant 10 J, transition state 50 J, product 5 J → Ea = 50 − 10 = 40 J, ΔH = 5 − 10 = −5 J (5 J released). Another: reactant 200 J, transition state 300 J → Ea = 100 J; if product = 160 J, ΔH = −40 J, exothermic.
  • Recognising exo/endo: (i) the reaction proceeds spontaneously without external energy → exothermic; (ii) product energy lower than reactant → exothermic; (iii) ΔH negative → exothermic. The opposite for endothermic.
  • The creative-style questions: (a) "State whether it is exothermic or endothermic", (b) "Draw the graph and indicate transition state, activated complex, reactant, product, Ea, ΔH", (c) "Find ΔH", (d) "Find the activation energy".

7 · Collision theory and transition-state theory

CLASSSLIDES 13–14
  • Collision theory: a reaction takes place only by collisions between reacting molecules, but not every collision gives a product; only a small fraction (effective collisions). Two conditions: (1) the molecules must have sufficient kinetic energy (≥ activation energy); (2) they must collide with the proper orientation. Slide: A–B + A–B colliding head-on is effective; a collision where the right atoms don't meet is ineffective even with high energy.
  • Transition-state theory: a collision does not directly cause reaction; the colliding molecules first form a transition state / activated complex which then decomposes to products. Steps: (1) fast-approaching molecules slow down because their electron clouds repel; kinetic energy is converted to potential energy; (2) the clouds interpenetrate, allowing rearrangement of valence electrons; (3) a partial bond forms (A···B) while the B–C bond weakens: the activated complex, momentary, which then gives A–B + C.
  • Orientation cannot be fully controlled from outside. Catalysts mostly speed reactions; a few (inhibitors) slow them.

8 · Effect of temperature and catalyst on the barrier

CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
  • To start the reaction the reactants need Ea (e.g. 40 J) but have only 10 J: they must "arrange" 40 J, which takes time. If you supply energy by raising the temperature (10 J → 40–50 J), they cross the barrier quickly.
  • Explain: raising the temperature increases the reactants' kinetic energy, so there are more effective collisions and the barrier is overcome more quickly. The activation energy does NOT decrease ("if you climb a mountain, can you cut it down? You energise yourself").
  • Concentration and pressure raise the rate by increasing effective collisions (pressure only for gases). Works the same way for exothermic or endothermic reactions.
  • Catalyst: gives an alternative route with lower Eₐ (the old path is unchanged); it also does not change ΔH.
  • Normally we only think about the forward reaction; every reaction is reversible in principle, but we stop after the product forms.

Also said in the tutorial class (same ideas, extra detail; may reappear)

  • Activation energy = the energy (not time) the reactants need to start the reaction: bonds must break and new ones form, and the outer electron clouds of colliding particles repel, so even a spontaneous reaction has a barrier at the start. Exothermic: the system supplies it itself; non-spontaneous/endothermic: energy must be supplied from outside (temperature, light), "otherwise the reaction never starts". Measure it from the reactant level ("many take it from the wrong place").
  • Catalyst: like choosing the easiest of many routes; the original route is not changed, the catalyst provides one with lower Ea. It can also add surface area (nanocatalysts) and weaken bonds. Typical catalysts: Ni, Fe, V₂O₅ (mostly d-block transition metals). A few catalysts are inhibitors (slow a reaction, e.g. to limit bacterial growth). Its main job is to speed up.
  • Temperature: Boltzmann distribution (number of molecules vs kinetic energy at a low and a high temperature; KE = ½mv²; higher T shifts the curve to higher KE). A barrier of 10 J with reactants raised from 10 J to 12 J: they cross more easily, but the barrier has not become smaller. Don't bring Le Chatelier's principle into this.
  • Pressure matters only for gases (more collisions); light: photochemical reactions (photons supply the activation energy, e.g. Cl₂ → 2Cl• radicals, photosynthesis); surface area: reaction occurs at the surface.
  • Temperature coefficient (slide 20): the ratio of the rate constants at two temperatures that differ by 10 °C.

9 · The Arrhenius equation

CLASSSLIDES 15–161–2 MARK MATHS / MCQ
  • Boltzmann's distribution (number of particles vs kinetic energy at two temperatures; a higher temperature shifts to higher energy) gave no equation. Arrhenius related the rate constant to temperature:
    k = A e−Ea/RT
  • k = rate constant · A = frequency factor (collisions per second; treated as constant) · Eₐ = activation energy (fixed for a reaction) · R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (given) · T = absolute temperature (K).
  • Only T and k change. Increase T → k increases → rate increases (rate ∝ k: if the rate doubles, k doubles).

Linear form and the graph

ln k = ln A − (Ea/R)(1/T)    (y = mx + c)
  • ln k (y) vs 1/T (x): straight line with negative slope (goes down). Slope = −Eₐ/R; intercept = ln A (so A is found from the intercept). A 1-mark question may ask for a tentative sketch.
  • Slope given: Eₐ = −slope × R. Slide example: slope = −1.2×10⁴ K → Eₐ = 8.314 × 1.2×10⁴ = 1.0×10⁵ J mol⁻¹. Unit: K cancels with R's K⁻¹, leaving J/mol. (When it says "slope", don't think of a tangent/instantaneous rate: think Arrhenius.)

Two temperatures (no graph)

ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) (1/T₁ − 1/T₂)    log(k₂/k₁) = Ea/(2.303R) · (T₂ − T₁)/(T₁T₂)
  • Derivation (not asked): write ln k₂ and ln k₁, subtract; A cancels (ratio); e's turn into ln; so you don't need A. The log form multiplies by 2.303 (log₁₀ vs ln). Logs are used to turn large or small numbers into manageable values.
  • T₂ is the higher temperature (with k₂); T₁ the lower. Write 1/T₁ first.
Pitfalls the lecturer sees
  1. Convert °C to K (+273): 620 °C → 893 K.
  2. Convert kJ/mol to J/mol (× 1000) because R is in J. (Ea = 10 kJ/mol → 10 000 J/mol.)
  3. Remove the ln operator before moving numbers. If ln(k₂/1.1) = −2, write k₂/1.1 = e^(−2) (Shift + ln on the calculator), then multiply by 1.1. Don't carry the 1.1 across the ln.
  4. If Ea is asked, you don't need A. If A is asked, an intercept (ln A) will be given.

Worked problems

Slide 15. 2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂; slope of ln k vs 1/T = −1.2×10⁴ K. Eₐ = 8.314 × 1.2×10⁴ = 9.98×10⁴ ≈ 1.0×10⁵ J mol⁻¹.

Slide 16. CH₄ + 2S₂ → CS₂ + 2H₂S. k = 1.1 L mol⁻¹s⁻¹ at 550 °C, 6.4 at 625 °C. T₁ = 823 K, T₂ = 898 K. ln(6.4/1.1) = 1.761. 1/T₁ − 1/T₂ = 1.015×10⁻⁴. Eₐ = 8.314 × 1.761/1.015×10⁻⁴ = 1.44×10⁵ J/mol (slide: 1.4×10⁵ J/mol).

Class question: "At 30 °C the rate is whatever; raise 10 °C and it doubles. Eₐ?" The rate is proportional to k, so k₂ = 2k₁. T₁ = 303 K, T₂ = 313 K. Eₐ = R ln 2/(1/303 − 1/313) = 8.314 × 0.6931/1.0544×10⁻⁴ = 5.47×10⁴ J mol⁻¹ (54.7 kJ/mol).

From the practice sheet. k₁ = 1.2 s⁻¹ at 320 K, Eₐ = 60 kJ/mol → k₂ at 290 K: ln(k₂/k₁) = (60000/8.314)(1/320 − 1/290) = −2.33 → k₂ = 0.116 s⁻¹. And k₂ = 2k₁, T₁ = 280 K, Eₐ = 65 kJ/mol → T₂ ≈ 287 K.
