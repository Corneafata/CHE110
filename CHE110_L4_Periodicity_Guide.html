What the lecturer said about the exam

  1. Periodicity comes as small sub-questions (1–1.5 marks) attached to bigger questions, and as MCQ / true-false. Quiz 2 and the midterm will have them.
  2. Explain with two terms: "In the exam I will specifically say: explain using shielding effect and effective nuclear charge why atomic radius decreases or increases group-wise or period-wise." Write 2–3 lines, not an essay.
  3. Write less, legibly, to the point. Space is deliberately small and "unnecessary writing" loses marks.
  4. True/false trap: "The radius of Cl⁻ is smaller than Cl" is False.
  5. Ion-size ordering questions "will definitely be in your exam": e.g. Al³⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻ and Fe⁴⁺ < Fe³⁺ < Fe²⁺.
  6. MCQ shortcut: for "highest ionization potential and lowest electron affinity", look for a noble gas among the options first. If it is there, that is the answer.
  7. Exceptions in ionization energy and electron affinity will be asked. Know the electronic configurations of atoms 1 to 20 (examples never go beyond 30). If asked to explain an exception, just show the configuration.
  8. Electronegativity exceptions (relativistic effect, Hg, Group 12/13) will not be asked. Know how EN works group-wise, period-wise and in bonding.
  9. Diagonal relationship: MCQs like "which pair is (not) diagonally related?" Never the numerical properties.
  10. Only what was taught in class is examined. The slides contain extra reading.

1 · Mendeleev and Moseley

CLASSSLIDES
  • Mendeleev (1869) ("father of the periodic table") arranged about 60-plus known elements by atomic mass, lowest to highest, grouped by matching properties. Protons, neutrons and electrons were not yet known, only masses.
  • It worked up to a limit but not for 100%: Ar (39.94) vs K (39.1) and Te (127.6) vs I (126.9). By mass, K and I should come first, but properties force Ar before K and Te before I. He placed them correctly but could not explain it.
  • His greater feat: he left blank spaces for elements he predicted; those elements were later found.
  • Moseley (1913) arranged elements by atomic (proton) number, which explains all the anomalies. That is the modern periodic table (118 elements, 7 periods, 18 groups). Rutherford had found only the nucleus, not separate proton and neutron numbers.
  • Modern periodic law: the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic number.

2 · Periodicity, trends and blocks

CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
  • Periodicity = repetition of properties at regular intervals with increasing atomic number. It means a gradual trend, not random jumps (10, 10.2, 10.5, 10.9 rather than 10, 20, 25, 12). Like periodic motion: after a fixed interval, the same pattern recurs.
  • Cause: the recurrence of similar valence-shell electronic configurations after regular intervals. Group 1 is always ns¹ (1s¹, 2s¹, 3s¹, 4s¹...). "Similar, not the same": the ns¹ part repeats, the shell number changes. A one-line answer: "similar valence configurations recur at fixed intervals".
  • Periodic trends: atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity. Periods = rows (7), groups = columns (18). Trends: across a period (left→right) and down a group (top→bottom).
  • Why it matters: an atom's tendency to give or take electrons (to reach stability, the octet) decides bonding; trends explain it.

Blocks (s, p, d, f)

BlockClass points
s-block (groups 1–2)Low melting and boiling points relative to d-block metals. Reason: only 1–2 valence electrons → weaker metallic bond. Melting/boiling does not break strong bonds (ionic, covalent, metallic); the distance between particles increases (weak interactions overcome, e.g. hydrogen bonds between water molecules).
d-block (mostly transition metals)Coloured compounds: ligands (e.g. H₂O, CN⁻) approach the metal ion and split the five degenerate d orbitals into a lower and a higher set (crystal-field splitting, not taught in detail). An electron absorbs a photon (hν) and jumps to the higher set, then falls back releasing visible light: the d–d transition. Examples: CuSO₄ blue; Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ reddish-brown. This is an "exception" because normally orbital type changes (s→p, p→d, d→f).
Alloys: an alloy mixes metals (e.g. steel, brass, duralumin). A d-block element is always present. The large d orbitals have vacancy/space where other metals' electrons can move in, giving a flexible structure and changed properties (strength). Zn is not a transition metal (full d¹⁰) but still forms alloys (brass with Cu).
f-blockMostly radioactive; coloured by f–f transitions but the colours are faded, because the f subshell is deep inside and the energy gap is small.
p-blockMostly non-metals.
Short-answer templates. "Why do d-block elements show colour?" → d–d transition between split d orbitals. "Why do d-block elements form alloys?" → vacant space in their d orbitals lets other metals' electrons enter.

3 · Shielding effect and effective nuclear charge

CLASSEXAM SIGNAL: use both terms
  • Nuclear charge Z = number of protons.
  • Effective nuclear charge Zeff = the net attraction of the nucleus on the valence-shell electrons (only the outermost shell).
    Zeff = Z − σ
    σ = shielding (screening) constant.
  • Shielding effect: inner-shell electrons (between the nucleus and the valence electrons) repel the outer electrons and reduce the attraction. Like a barrier weakening a field. s and p electrons are dense and compact; d and f orbitals are diffuse and shield poorly.
  • Down a group: Z rises, but shells are added one by one, so σ rises too. Zeff increases only a little (almost constant). Example values the lecturer gave only for illustration: Z = 1, 3, 11 with Zeff = 0.5, 0.75, 1.
  • Across a period: same number of shells, so σ is almost constant, so Zeff increases rapidly.
  • "Balanced" picture used in class: 11 protons balance 1 valence electron in Na, 12 balance 2 in Mg, 13 balance 3 in Al, 17 balance 7 in Cl. Fewer electrons than this balance → stronger pull per electron; extra electrons → repulsion and expansion.
Model answer, group-wise (2–3 lines). "Down a group the number of shells increases, so the shielding effect increases. The effective nuclear charge therefore does not increase proportionally, the attraction on the outermost electrons decreases, and the atomic radius increases."
Model answer, period-wise. "Across a period the shielding effect is almost constant (same shells) but the nuclear charge increases, so Zeff increases and pulls the valence electrons closer; the radius decreases."

4 · Atomic and ionic radius

CLASSSLIDESEXAM SIGNAL
  • Radius = distance from the nucleus to the outermost shell where electrons are found. It is not a hard wall: "as far as electrons are found". So the same atom in different charge states has different radii.
  • Types (slide): covalent radius (non-metals): half the distance between the nuclei of two covalently bonded atoms. Metallic/crystal radius (metals): half the distance between adjacent nuclei in the closed-packed lattice. Ionic radius: distance from the nucleus to the outermost shell of an ion.
  • Trends: across a period ↓ (Zeff ↑); down a group ↑ (shells added, shielding ↑).

Cations (always smaller than the parent atom)

  • Remove the outermost electron(s): the outer shell may vanish (Na → Na⁺, Mg → Mg²⁺), and even when it does not (Mg → Mg⁺ still has one 3s electron), 12 protons now pull fewer electrons → Zeff increases, the cloud shrinks.
  • The higher the charge, the smaller: Mg²⁺ < Mg⁺ < Mg; Al³⁺ < Al²⁺ < Al⁺ < Al; Fe⁴⁺ < Fe³⁺ < Fe²⁺ < Fe⁺ < Fe.
  • When explaining a cation, say "Zeff increases" (shielding is not the point because the number of shells is the same).

Anions (always larger than the parent atom)

  • Cl (2,8,7) → Cl⁻ (2,8,8): 17 protons balanced 7 valence electrons; an extra electron enters 3p (next to an electron already there). Electron–electron repulsion; the electron cloud spreads out so the electrons keep apart. The electron does not go to a new shell.
  • The more negative the charge, the larger: O²⁻ > F⁻ (same electrons, fewer protons). F ≈ 64 pm → F⁻ ≈ 133 pm (example values).
  • True/false: "The radius of Cl⁻ is smaller than Cl" is False. (Rare exceptions exist, but in general anion > atom.)

Isoelectronic species

Same number of electrons (and so the same shells) → compare by nuclear charge: the more protons, the stronger the pull, the smaller the species.
Al³⁺ (13 p) < Na⁺ (11 p) < F⁻ (9 p) < O²⁻ (8 p)   (all 10 e⁻)
Cations are the smallest, then anions in order of increasing negative charge. There are no exceptions in this.

5 · Ionization energy (ionization potential)

CLASSSLIDESEXAM SIGNAL
  • Definition: energy needed to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom (to make a cation). Endothermic: energy must be supplied because the electron is bound in its shell by attraction.
  • Successive IE: electrons leave one by one. IE₃ > IE₂ > IE₁ (the slide says IE₂ is always greater than IE₁). Each time the ion is smaller with higher Zeff; e.g. removing an electron from Al²⁺ is hardest. Trends are compared using the first ionization energy.
  • Group-wise: IE decreases: shielding ↑, Zeff hardly rises, size ↑, so the outer electron is easy to remove (Na is easier than Li: Li is smaller and holds its electron more strongly).
  • Period-wise: IE increases: Zeff ↑ with no new shell, so Na loses an electron easily and Cl does not.

Exceptions (half-filled and full-filled stability)

  • Stable arrangements: full-filled (s², p⁶, d¹⁰, f¹⁴; noble gases) and half-filled (p³, d⁵, f⁷). Such atoms resist losing (and gaining) electrons. Example: Cu = 3d¹⁰4s¹ instead of 4s²3d⁹.
  • N (2p³) > O (2p⁴). N is half-filled. Configurations: N 1s²2s²2p³, O 1s²2s²2p⁴.
  • Be (2s²) > B (2p¹) (full-filled 2s). On the slide also Mg > Al and P > S.
  • On an IE vs Z graph the IE rises across each period but dips at B and at O (second period) and again at Al and S (third period); noble gases are the peaks. Each period's noble gas has the highest IE in that period.
MCQ tip: "Which has the highest ionization potential and lowest electron affinity?" → look for a noble gas among the options.

6 · Electron affinity

CLASSSLIDESEXAM SIGNAL
  • Definition: energy released when a neutral, isolated gaseous atom accepts an electron to form an anion. It is a free electron being attracted ("kind of love: you pull it in"), so no energy has to be supplied. Free electron at zero energy; once in an orbit it releases energy and goes negative (more stable). Affinity measures how strongly/quickly the atom pulls the electron in.
  • Period-wise: increases (Zeff ↑). Group-wise: decreases (larger size; the nucleus reaches the free electron less effectively).
  • Period exceptions (half/full-filled): C > N (N is 2p³, half-filled and does not want another electron). On the slide also Si > P. Noble gases ≈ 0 (full p subshell).
  • Group exception (group 17): Cl > F > Br > I. F is so small that the very high electron density on its surface repels the incoming electron even though the nucleus attracts it.
  • If asked to explain an exception, just show the configuration (e.g. N = 2p³, half-filled).

7 · Electronegativity

CLASSSLIDESEXAM SIGNAL
  • Definition: the tendency of an atom to attract the shared (bonded) pair of electrons in a covalent bond (e.g. Cl pulls electron density in HCl; F in HF).
  • Electron affinity vs electronegativity: free electron (energy released) vs bonded electron (a relative value on a scale, the Pauling scale; not an energy value like IE/EA).
  • It does not depend on half- or full-filled stability (the electron is already in a bond). It depends on Zeff, shielding, size and s/p character (slide: s-character: C–C < C=C < C≡C).
  • Trends: ↑ across a period, ↓ down a group (size ↑). F is highest (4.0), Cs lowest. Slide Pauling values: F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0, Cl 3.1. Higher oxidation number → higher EN (Fe < Fe²⁺ < Fe³⁺).
  • Oxidation numbers: in OF₂, F = −1 so O = +2; in Cl₂O₇, O = −2 so Cl = +7. Two non-metals can bond with the less electronegative one positive when the other is very electronegative; the structure remains stable.
  • Oxides across a period (slide): Na₂O (strongly basic) → Al₂O₃ (amphoteric) → Cl₂O₇ (strongly acidic): chemical properties are periodic too.
  • Not examined: exceptions in groups 12/13 (Zn, Cd, Hg; Al, Ga, Tl). In class the explanation was the relativistic effect (heavy nucleus → fast 1s electrons → mass increases → s shells contract) plus poor shielding by d/f orbitals, so Zeff rises. It also explains why Hg is liquid (weak metallic bonding). Understanding it is a bonus, not required.

8 · Diagonal relationship

CLASSSLIDESMCQ / TRUE-FALSE SIGNAL
  • An element of period 2 resembles the element one group to the right in period 3 (diagonally down-right). Reason: radius decreases across a period but increases down a group, so the effects partly cancel.
  • Pairs: Li – Mg, Be – Al, B – Si. (C – P also fits the pattern: 4 valence electrons ↔ 5 in the next period. C and S are not diagonally related.)
  • Li and Mg similarities (slide, don't memorise): both react directly with N₂ to form nitrides; fluorides, carbonates and phosphates are insoluble; both are hard metals; hydroxides are weak bases; strong metallic bond, high melting and boiling points.
  • Questions ask which pair is or is not diagonally related, never the numerical properties.

9 · Defects of the periodic table

CLASSSLIDES
  1. Position of hydrogen: resembles alkali metals (1 valence electron, forms H⁺) and halogens (gains an electron to form H⁻, exists as diatomic H₂), yet is unique (small, light, forms hydrogen bonds). Hydrogen is on one side and helium far on the other.
  2. Lanthanides and actinides (57–71, 89–103) are placed separately below the table, because 28 more inline elements would make the table 32 columns wide. This hides their true positions between groups 3 and 4 and their similarity with d-block elements.
  3. No fixed position for isotopes: same atomic number, different mass (C-12 and C-14), same place, but different properties; the table does not distinguish them.
  4. No sharp boundary between metals, non-metals and metalloids: the zigzag line is not clean (As, Sb show mixed properties).

The lecturer's remark: just mention a few; even with these flaws the table is hugely useful.

Cheat sheet

History: Mendeleev (mass, 1869, left gaps) → Moseley (atomic number, 1913). Ar/K, Te/I anomalies.
Periodicity cause: recurrence of similar valence configurations.
Zeff = Z − σ (valence electrons only).
Group ↓: shielding ↑, Zeff ~ constant → radius ↑, IE ↓, EA ↓, EN ↓.
Period →: shielding ~ constant, Zeff ↑ → radius ↓, IE ↑, EA ↑, EN ↑.
Cation < atom; anion > atom. Isoelectronic: more protons = smaller.
IE exceptions: N > O, Be > B (Mg > Al, P > S). Noble gas: highest IE.
EA exceptions: C > N, Cl > F > Br > I, noble gas ≈ 0 (lowest).
EN: bonded electron, no half/full effect, F highest.
EA = free e⁻ (energy released); IE = energy supplied (endothermic).
Diagonal: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si (C–P).
d-block: colour = d–d transition; alloys = vacant d space; s-block low mp = weak metallic bond.
Defects: H, lanthanides/actinides, isotopes, metal/non-metal boundary.
