Must-know facts (one screen per lecture)

Lecture 4: Periodicity

  • Mendeleev: atomic mass (1869), left gaps; Moseley: atomic number (1913). Ar/K, Te/I anomalies. Modern law: properties are periodic functions of atomic number. Cause: recurrence of similar valence configurations.
  • Zeff = Z − σ. Down a group: shielding ↑, Zeff hardly ↑ → radius ↑, IE ↓, EA ↓, EN ↓. Across a period: shielding ≈ constant, Zeff ↑ → radius ↓, IE ↑, EA ↑, EN ↑.
  • Cation < atom (Mg²⁺ < Mg⁺ < Mg; Fe⁴⁺ < Fe³⁺ < Fe²⁺). Anion > atom (Cl⁻ > Cl). Isoelectronic: Al³⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻.
  • IE endothermic; IE₃ > IE₂ > IE₁; exceptions N > O, Be > B (Mg > Al, P > S). Noble gas = highest IE, lowest EA.
  • EA = energy released for a free electron; exceptions C > N, Cl > F > Br > I. EN = bonded electron, no half/full effect; F highest.
  • Diagonal: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si (C–P). d-block: colour (d–d transition), alloys (vacant d space). s-block: low mp (weak metallic bond). Defects: H, lanthanides/actinides, isotopes, metal/non-metal boundary.

Lecture 5–6 (slide 23+): Rate law and order

  • Rate = k[A]^x[B]^y; write "Rate ="; reactants only; powers experimental (coefficients only if elementary); overall order = sum.
  • Rate vs [A]: 0 horizontal; 1 line through origin (slope = k); 2 parabola.
  • Zero: [A] = [A]₀ − kt, t½ = [A]₀/2k, unit M s⁻¹, k = rate; first: ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt, t½ = 0.693/k, unit s⁻¹; second: 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt, t½ = 1/(k[A]₀), unit M⁻¹ s⁻¹.
  • Order from data: ratio of two experiments with one concentration fixed. ×2 → ×2: first; ×2 → ×4: second; unchanged: zero. Percentage problems: use the remaining amount.
  • Radioactive decay = first order. Zero order: average = instantaneous = initial rate.

Lecture 7: Mechanism, energy diagrams, Arrhenius

  • Elementary = single step, powers = coefficients. Molecularity = number of reactant particles in an elementary step (whole, ≥ 1, theoretical); order experimental, can be 0 or fractional. High molecularity rare (simultaneous effective collisions improbable).
  • RDS = slowest step (True). Intermediate: made then consumed (NO₃ in N₂O₅). Composite types: parallel, reversible, consecutive.
  • PE diagram: Ea = TS − reactant; ΔH = product − reactant; exo (ΔH < 0, product lower, spontaneous), endo (ΔH > 0, reactant more stable). TS/activated complex = highest, unstable.
  • Temperature raises kinetic energy, not lowers Ea; catalyst lowers Ea by a new path. Collision theory: energy + orientation.
  • Arrhenius: k = Ae^(−Ea/RT); ln k vs 1/T slope −Ea/R, intercept ln A; ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂); K, J, remove ln first.

Carry-over: the tutorial class and slides 17–22 (safety net)

The announcement says slide 23 onwards for Lecture 5–6, but the lecturer once said "slide 20 or 23", Lecture 7 builds on these ideas, and the same points were repeated in the tutorial class. If a question borrows from them it will use her wording below. Details: Tutorial guide.

Factors affecting the rate (six)
  • Concentration, pressure (gases only), temperature, catalyst, light, surface area.
  • Concentration/pressure/temperature: more (effective) collisions. Temperature raises kinetic energy; it does not lower Ea. Too much concentration "leaves no space": surface area matters too.
  • Catalyst: alters the rate without being consumed; new route with lower Ea; regenerated; Ni, Fe, V₂O₅ (transition metals).
  • Light: photochemical reactions; photons give the activation energy; Cl₂ → 2Cl•.
  • Surface area: reaction at the surface (chopped wood, nanomaterials).
  • Temperature coefficient (slide 20): ratio of k at two temperatures 10 °C apart.
Rates and graphs (tutorial)
  • Three rates: initial (t = 0; used for order), average (ΔC/Δt over the given interval), instantaneous (tangent slope).
  • Reactant curve: steepest at the start (more reactant → more effective collisions), then flatter, then almost flat (completion/equilibrium). Product curve starts at zero.
  • Rate of disappearance of A = |d[A]/dt|; reaction rate = that ÷ coefficient; rates are positive (modulus).
  • Straight-line graph: average = instantaneous rate (zero order).
  • Exam habits: units, big freehand graph, don't force the curve through odd points, tangent touches only the chosen point.

Model short answers (2–3 lines each)

Write like this: direct, to the point, legible. Tap a question to see the answer.

Why does the atomic radius increase down a group? (use shielding effect and Zeff)

Shells are added one by one, so the shielding effect increases. Zeff therefore cannot increase proportionally to Z, the attraction on the valence electrons weakens, and the radius increases.

Why does the atomic radius decrease across a period?

The number of shells is the same, so shielding is almost constant, while the nuclear charge increases. Zeff increases, pulling the valence electrons closer, so the radius decreases.

Why is a cation smaller and an anion larger than its parent atom?

Cation: fewer electrons (an outer shell may vanish), so Zeff increases and the remaining electrons are pulled closer. Anion: the extra electron repels the existing ones; the cloud expands to reduce repulsion (Zeff per electron falls).

Arrange Al³⁺, Na⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ in increasing size and explain.

Al³⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻. They are isoelectronic (10 electrons), so the one with more protons (higher nuclear charge) holds the electrons more tightly and is smaller.

Explain the ionization-energy trend and an exception.

IE decreases down a group (more shielding, larger size) and increases across a period (Zeff increases). Exception: N (2p³, half-filled) has higher IE than O (2p⁴); Be (2s², full) > B.

Why is the electron affinity of F less than that of Cl?

F is very small; the high electron density on its surface repels the incoming electron, so Cl releases more energy (Cl > F > Br > I).

Difference between electron affinity and electronegativity.

Electron affinity: energy released when a free gaseous atom accepts an electron. Electronegativity: tendency of an atom to attract the shared (bonded) electron pair in a covalent bond; a relative value (Pauling scale).

Why do transition metal compounds show colour? Why do they form alloys?

Colour: ligands split the d orbitals; an electron absorbs a photon and makes a d–d transition. Alloys: their large d orbitals have vacant space to accommodate other metals' electrons.

What is the cause of periodicity?

The recurrence of similar valence-shell electronic configurations at regular intervals of atomic number (e.g. ns¹ for the alkali metals).

Name two defects of the periodic table.

Hydrogen has no proper position (resembles groups 1 and 17); lanthanides and actinides are placed separately; no place for isotopes; no sharp boundary between metals and non-metals.

Write the rate law for 2NO + H₂ → products (order not given); find the overall order if x = 2, y = 1.

Rate = k[NO]^x[H₂]^y; overall order = x + y = 3. Only reactants appear; the powers aren't the coefficients (not stated as elementary).

Draw rate vs concentration for zero, first and second order.

Rate on y, [A] on x. Zero: horizontal line. First: straight line through the origin (slope = k). Second: parabola. (Draw all three on one graph.)

How can you identify the order from the unit of k?

s⁻¹ → first; mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (same as the rate) → zero; L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ → second.

Show that the half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of concentration.

ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt with [A] = [A]₀/2 gives ln 2 = kt½, so t½ = 0.693/k: no concentration term.

Why are radioactive decays first order?

One particle breaks down on its own in a single step; no collisions are involved, so concentration or temperature don't change the decay rate.

Why are reactions of high molecularity rare?

A simultaneous collision of many particles with sufficient energy and correct orientation is very improbable, so most reactions of molecularity 4 or more don't occur; they proceed in steps.

Write two differences between molecularity and order.

Order is experimental (from the rate law), molecularity is theoretical (from the elementary step). Order can be zero or fractional; molecularity is always a positive whole number.

What is the rate-determining step? What is an intermediate?

RDS: the slowest step of a mechanism, controlling the overall rate. Intermediate: a species formed in one step and consumed in a later one; it doesn't appear in the net equation (e.g. NO₃).

An exothermic reaction: draw the PE diagram and label it.

Reactants at the higher level, rising to the peak (transition state = activated complex), then falling to a lower product level. Ea = peak − reactant level (from the reactant). ΔH = product − reactant < 0.

Does raising the temperature lower the activation energy?

No. Ea is fixed. Higher temperature increases the reactants' kinetic energy, giving more effective collisions that overcome the barrier faster.

How does a catalyst increase the rate?

It provides an alternative path with lower activation energy and is regenerated. The old path and ΔH are unchanged.

Two conditions for an effective collision.

Sufficient kinetic energy (at least the activation energy) and proper orientation.

State the Arrhenius equation; what is plotted and what is the slope?

k = A e^(−Ea/RT). ln k vs 1/T is a straight line with slope −Ea/R and intercept ln A.
