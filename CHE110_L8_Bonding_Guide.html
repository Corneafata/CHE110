What the lecturer said about the exam

  1. "This graph comes in your exam very often." You may be asked to draw (or explain) the potential energy vs internuclear distance curve for H–H or H–Cl and mark: the most stable point, bond energy, bond length, the repulsive region and the attractive region. You must draw the zero line and label the axes (internuclear distance on x; positive and negative potential energy on y).
  2. Bond formation = exothermic and bond breaking = endothermic ("these come in your exam").
  3. Lattice energy comparisons come as short questions: order several ionic compounds and give the reason (charge or size). Don't bring in electronegativity or electron affinity ("I have to Google your answers").
  4. Force on a crystal: "Magnesium chloride crystal, HCl and a magnesium strip: force is applied to all three. What changes happen and why?" Ionic: breaks (like charges face each other and repel). Covalent: little change (gas/liquid/soft). Metal: deforms but doesn't break (electrons rearrange quickly).
  5. Recognise ionic vs covalent from the formula (metal + non-metal; polyatomic ions behave like a metal/non-metal). NH₄Cl is ionic; sulfuryl chloride is covalent. A question may only say "ammonium chloride crystal".
  6. Solubility and conductivity: "Will NaCl dissolve in CCl₄?" (true/false trap: CCl₄ is non-polar). "Will ammonium chloride crystal conduct electricity?" (not as a solid; yes if molten or aqueous).
  7. Hydrogen bonding: "Whose boiling point is abnormally high, water or H₂S, and why?" (intermolecular hydrogen bonding); "Why does ice float on water?"; given a structure, say whether the hydrogen bond is intermolecular or intramolecular (true/false). Draw the hydrogen bond as the dotted line, not the covalent bond.
  8. Lone pair electrons are not free electrons (a student wrote "has a lone pair so it conducts").
  9. Only what was taught in class: "if you study the properties I taught well, that's enough; don't memorise the slides line by line". Slide structures needn't be drawn.

1 · Why do atoms form bonds? The potential-energy curve

CLASSSLIDES 3–5EXAM SIGNAL: DRAW IT
  • Chemical bond = the attractive force that holds two or more atoms together in a molecule or ion; atoms interact by losing, gaining or sharing electrons to reach a stable outer shell.
  • Three reasons (slide): (1) a net attractive force between atoms (attraction and repulsion between nuclei and electrons; the net can be either); (2) the octet rule (noble-gas outer shell of 8; the duplet of 2 for H and He); (3) lowering of energy: the combining atoms' potential energy decreases and they become more stable.
  • Class explanation: stability = lower potential energy (the lower, even below zero, the more stable). An isolated hydrogen atom is reactive, "fighting with whoever it meets"; as H₂ its potential energy is lower. Gold, platinum and the noble gases are fine as single atoms (rarely bond). Most others bond: metals as metallic lattices; non-metals as diatomic molecules or S₈, P₄.
  • For a bond to start, atoms need an attractive pull (tendency to fill the octet/duplet). Atoms far apart (Chittagong and Dhaka) don't bond.

The graph (potential energy vs internuclear distance, r)

  1. Large r: free atoms, no interaction, PE = 0 (zero line).
  2. As the atoms approach, attraction dominates and PE falls below zero: energy is released.
  3. Closer still, the electron clouds (and nuclei) repel; repulsion rises until it equals the attraction. At that point the PE is at its minimum: the most stable state; the bond is formed. Tug-of-war analogy: equal pulls, so the ribbon doesn't move.
  4. Bond length = the internuclear distance at the minimum ("an optimum distance, not a minimum distance"; the atoms vibrate about it).
  5. Bond energy = the depth of the minimum below zero: the energy released on forming the bond = the energy needed to break it.
  6. Closer than the bond length: repulsion dominates: PE shoots up on the positive side (repulsive region; bond-breaking by squeezing, "one on another's shoulders"). Farther than the bond length (up to where PE returns to 0): the attractive region.
Bond formation is exothermic (energy released); bond breaking is endothermic (energy supplied). Slide example: H₂ has bond energy 103.2 kcal/mol and bond length 0.74 Å.
In the exam draw: the zero line (x-axis), the vertical PE axis with positive and negative sides, a curve starting at zero at large r, dipping to a minimum and rising steeply at small r. Label: most stable point/minimum, bond length (r₀), bond energy, attractive region, repulsive region. You can show all in one graph; "definitely denote the axes".

2 · The ionic bond

CLASSSLIDES 8–15EXAM SIGNAL
  • Definition: the bond formed by transfer of one or more valence electrons from one atom to another: the electrostatic attraction between cation (+) and anion (−). Metals form cations (lose electrons); non-metals form anions (gain electrons). Cations and anions in two separate beakers don't form a bond: it is the attraction between them that makes the bond (and the bond becomes stable when attraction and repulsion balance).
  • Forming the ions: removing an electron needs energy (ionization energy, endothermic), so it should be low for the metal. Adding an electron releases energy (electron affinity), so it should be high for the non-metal. Don't mix up four properties: metals have low IE and low EA (they give electrons away); non-metals have high IE and high EA (they won't give electrons; they attract them). Think with Na and Cl: Na gives its single electron easily; Cl, with 7 electrons, gladly takes one.
  • Slide conditions: (1) atom A has 1, 2 or 3 valence electrons and atom B has 5, 6 or 7; (2) net lowering of energy (low IE, high EA, high lattice energy); (3) electronegativity difference of about 2 or more (Na 0.9, Cl 3.0: difference 2.1).
  • Lattice energy (also called electrical energy): the energy released when one mole of an ionic compound forms from its gaseous ions. The greater the lattice energy, the stronger the ionic bond.

Lattice energy: charge and size (and Coulomb's law)

F ∝ q₁q₂ / r²
  • Higher charge → stronger attraction → higher lattice energy.
  • Smaller size (of the cation or the anion) → smaller r → higher lattice energy.
  • NaCl vs MgCl₂: MgCl₂ is higher. Chlorine is common, so compare the metals: Mg²⁺ has the higher charge (higher Zeff) and is smaller than Na⁺. In the Coulomb picture: charges 2 and 2 (two Cl⁻) vs 1 and 1.
  • NaF vs NaCl: NaF is higher because the F⁻ ion is smaller. Don't say "F has higher electron affinity than Cl": the ions already exist, so affinity no longer matters ("it's the pulling now").
  • MgCl₂ vs AlCl₃: AlCl₃ is higher: charges 3 and 3 vs 2 and 2, and Al is smaller than Mg.
  • The transcript compares these in pairs. If all appear together, charge matters most: MgCl₂ (≈2526 kJ/mol) > NaF (≈923) > NaCl (≈787).
  • Exam: you get a list and are asked to order them and sometimes to explain: use charge or size, in one or two lines.

Properties of ionic compounds

PropertyReason (class)
Solids at room temperature; crystal latticeStrong electrostatic forces lock the ions in a regular 3D crystal lattice (+ − + − in all directions); no freedom of movement. Soft in some cases but mostly solid.
High melting and boiling pointsMany ions in a lattice; the ions need much more kinetic energy to separate (higher temperature).
Hard but brittleLayers of alternating + and − ions: force shifts one layer so that like charges come face to face and repel; the crystal cleaves along a plane. (Write: "like charges come close, repulsion increases, it breaks".)
Soluble in waterWater is polar: it attracts the ions and pulls them out of the lattice ("like dissolves like"). Slide: hydration energy (energy released when ions are solvated) compensates the lattice energy.
Insoluble in non-polar solventsCCl₄ (polar bonds cancel, so it is non-polar) and benzene (C₆H₆) can't separate the ions: low dielectric constant. True/false trap: "NaCl dissolves in CCl₄" is False.
ConductivityConduction needs free electrons or mobile ions. Solid: ions fixed → no. Molten or aqueous: ions are free → yes. Answer with the state: "not as a solid, yes if molten or dissolved".
Ionic reactions are fast (slide)Reactions between ions are very fast; molecular (covalent) reactions are slow.

Recognising ionic compounds

  • Ionic = metal + non-metal. S, O, Cl are all non-metals → covalent (sulfuryl chloride, SO₂Cl₂).
  • Polyatomic ions (radicals) act as one unit: ammonium (NH₄⁺) acts like a metal; sulfate, carbonate, phosphate, nitrate act as anions. So NH₄Cl (and Na₂SO₄, CaCO₃) are ionic even though all atoms in NH₄Cl are non-metals. Inside the ion the bonds are covalent. "Only the common ones are asked; nothing uncommon."
  • If a question says "ammonium chloride crystal" you should understand it is ionic just from the name.

3 · The covalent bond

CLASSSLIDES 16–20EXAM SIGNAL
  • Definition: the attractive force between atoms resulting from sharing an electron pair (each atom contributes one electron and has an equal claim on the pair). Formed between non-metals.
  • Conditions (class + slide): (1) the atoms taking part must have unpaired electrons (a pair already filled won't bond); (2) they are non-metals of equal or nearly equal electronegativity with 5, 6 or 7 valence electrons (H has one and attains a duplet); (3) while bonding the shared electrons must have opposite spins (e.g. H 1s¹ + 1s¹ overlap, one up and one down), so the clouds rearrange without repelling.
  • Octet filling happens after the bond is formed, it is not the condition for participating.

Properties

PropertyReason
Gases, liquids or soft solids at room temperature; no ionic crystal latticeAtoms in a molecule are firmly held, but molecules attract each other only by weak (van der Waals) forces.
Low and variable melting/boiling pointsWeak intermolecular forces: easy to separate molecules.
Applying force: not much change (neither hard nor brittle)No layers of ions; layers slip with no repulsion.
Non-conductorsNo ions and no free electrons. Water is a poor conductor (very little dissociation into H⁺ and OH⁻). Lone pair electrons are not free electrons.
Solubility (slide)Generally soluble in non-polar organic solvents (benzene, ether); insoluble in water, except some (alcohols, amines) that hydrogen bond with water. Class rule: polar dissolves polar, non-polar dissolves non-polar.
Isomerism; slow molecular reactions (slide)Rigid, directional bonds allow spatial arrangements (stereoisomerism).

Lone pairs vs free electrons

Localised: the electron is in a specific orbital of one atom (e.g. the lone pair on nitrogen after it forms 3 bonds, 5 − 3 = 2 left). Delocalised (free): not in any specific orbital or atom; it roams the vacant orbitals. A student once wrote "it has a lone pair, so it conducts": wrong. Lone pairs stay on the atom (shown as dots); bonding pairs are shown as lines.

Sigma and pi bonds (slides 19–20)

  • Sigma (σ): end-to-end (head-on, axial) overlap along the internuclear axis: s–s (H₂), s–p (HF, HCl), p–p (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂). Pi (π): sidewise (lateral) overlap of half-filled orbitals perpendicular to the axis. Sigma is stronger than pi.
  • Polar covalent bond: electrons shared unequally; the bonded atoms acquire partial positive and negative charges (δ⁺, δ⁻). The greater the electronegativity difference, the greater the polarity.
  • Both are "look through" (the lecturer said to read them; more detail wasn't taught).

4 · Coordinate covalent (dative) bond

CLASSSLIDES 21–22
  • In a normal covalent bond each atom gives one electron. In a coordinate covalent (dative) bond both electrons of the shared pair come from one atom: the donor (has a lone pair) → the acceptor (has an empty orbital or an incomplete octet). It is drawn with an arrow from donor to acceptor.
  • Why does it form? Boron in BF₃ has only 3 valence electrons (6 around it): an octet deficiency, so it is electron-seeking (an electrophile) and bonds to a lone pair, e.g. from NH₃ (N has the lone pair): NH₃ → BF₃ complex.
  • Examples: NH₄⁺ (NH₃ donates its lone pair to H⁺, which has no electron), H₃O⁺ (H₂O donates to H⁺), BF₄⁻, NH₃–BCl₃ addition compound, nitromethane (CH₃NO₂), Al₂Cl₆, ozone (O₃).
  • After formation, you cannot tell where the electrons came from: it behaves just like a normal covalent bond (all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are the same).
  • So the four strong bond types studied: covalent, ionic, metallic, coordinate covalent.

5 · The metallic bond

CLASSSLIDES 27–29EXAM SIGNAL
  • Ionic, covalent and metallic bonds are strong, atom-to-atom (or atom-to-electron) interactions, not molecule-to-molecule.
  • Electron-sea model: metal atoms give up their valence electrons (low ionization energy); the electrons become delocalised and move in the vacant orbitals of the surrounding metal ions (e.g. Mg 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁰ has vacant 3p orbitals). The positive ions sit in a 3D lattice (a crystal), "immersed in a sea of electrons". The attraction between the cations (higher Zeff after losing electrons) and the delocalised electrons is the metallic bond.
  • Delocalised electrons stay in the region of nearby atoms; "free" means free within that region, not travelling across the whole piece.

Properties (with the class reasons)

PropertyReason
Electrical conductivityMobile electrons are displaced when a voltage is applied. Electrons pass the energy/push to their neighbours; they don't run the whole length of the wire (like balls in a tube: push one in, another comes out).
Heat conductivityElectrons absorb heat, vibrate more, collide with neighbours and pass the energy along (aluminium pots, copper wires).
Ductile and malleableWhen one layer is forced across another the electron sea rearranges quickly, so like charges (cation–cation) don't come together and the lattice isn't broken; the metal deforms instead of breaking (unlike an ionic crystal). Electrons are tiny and can move through vacant orbitals; a chloride ion can't.
Luster (slide)Delocalised electrons absorb light, jump up and fall back, re-emitting light of the same frequency, seen as reflection.
Electron emission (slide)Enough heat or light energy removes an outer electron: photoelectric effect (Einstein's Nobel Prize).
Class question: "force is applied to MgCl₂ crystal, HCl, and a Mg strip". MgCl₂ (ionic crystal): layers shift, like charges come close, repulsion increases, it breaks (draw the layers shifting). HCl (covalent; gas or liquid, at most soft solid): no real application of force; little change. Mg (metallic): deformation (don't say "no change"); it doesn't break, because the negatively charged electrons rearrange very fast so + and + (or − and −) are never brought together.

6 · The hydrogen bond

CLASSSLIDES 23–26EXAM SIGNAL
  • The earlier bonds are strong and atom-to-atom. The hydrogen bond is a weaker molecule-to-molecule interaction (the major covalent bond has already formed). Drawn as a dotted line ("not a real bond").
  • How: in HF, F (electronegativity 4.0 on the Pauling scale) pulls the shared pair so strongly that F becomes δ⁻ and H δ⁺ (a polar molecule, a dipole). The δ⁺ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ⁻ F of the next. Since hydrogen is common to this interaction, it is a hydrogen bond. Polar molecules interact pole-to-pole.
  • Conditions (slide): (1) hydrogen bonded to a highly electronegative atom: F, O or N (HF, H₂O, NH₃); (2) the electronegative atom must be small: the smaller, the stronger the pull and the H-bond. N and Cl both have EN 3.0, but H-bonding is more effective in NH₃ than in HCl.
  • H₂S does not form H-bonds (S is larger, less electronegative). Chlorine only weakly.
  • It is directional (like a covalent bond): H bonds from specific directions.
Common mistake: in a drawing, the solid line between H and F (or O) is the covalent bond. The hydrogen bond is the dotted line from H to the neighbouring molecule's F/O/N.

Intermolecular vs intramolecular

  • Intermolecular: between different molecules (HF, H₂O, NH₃).
  • Intramolecular: within one molecule between two groups of the same molecule (2-nitrophenol, 2-nitrobenzoic acid, salicylaldehyde). A question may give a structure and ask (true/false) which type it can form: look at whether the + and − parts are inside the same molecule. "Inter-university vs intra-university" analogy.
  • Effect on properties: intermolecular H-bonding raises melting and boiling points abnormally: extra energy must break the interactions between molecules when they separate (distance increases on melting/boiling). Intramolecular H-bonding has no effect on melting point, boiling point, viscosity, surface tension, solubility.

Water vs H₂S, H₂Se, H₂Te (exam question)

  • Down group 16 the size and mass of the molecule rise, so boiling points should rise (H₂O should be the lowest). Instead water's is abnormally high, because of intermolecular hydrogen bonding that must be broken. The other hydrides can't H-bond. The graph (boiling point vs molar mass) shows water far above the trend (same for HF and NH₃ in their groups).

Why does ice float on water? (exam question)

In ice each water molecule is bonded to two H atoms by covalent bonds and to two others by hydrogen bonds (tetrahedral arrangement, each molecule linked to four). This builds an open hexagonal cage with a lot of vacant space, so the volume increases and the density decreases: ice floats. In liquid water the hydrogen bonds form randomly (no 3D cage), so the density is higher. Write the term "intermolecular hydrogen bonding"; otherwise the reason isn't clear. (A bottle of water cracks or its cap tightens on freezing because the volume increases.)

Other characteristics (slide): high solubility of some covalent compounds containing O, N, F in H-containing solvents (NH₃ and CH₃OH dissolve in water); hydrogen bonds are directional and strong enough to build a 3D lattice.

How to write the answers (the lecturer's rules)

  • Space is small: answer, then one line of reason. Don't copy the question. If it is yes/no, write "Yes" or "No" and go straight to the reason.
  • Divide the space for the number of parts. No four-page essays; "write only as much as asked, with the reason".
  • Maths: write the formula, substitute, give the answer with the unit (0.25 for the unit). Explain how you got the order in one line.
  • Read the whole true/false statement; if half is false, the whole is false.
  • Think with an example (Na and Cl); don't change answers because of what the person next to you says.
  • She checks your rough work and gives 0.5 for a good attempt.

Cheat sheet

PE curve: x = internuclear distance, y = PE, zero line = free atoms; minimum = bond formed (attraction = repulsion); r₀ = bond length; depth = bond energy; left = repulsive, right = attractive. Formation exothermic, breaking endothermic.
Ionic: metal + non-metal; low IE, high EA; lattice energy ↑ with charge ↑ and size ↓ (q₁q₂/r²); solid, high mp, brittle; water-soluble, not in CCl₄/benzene; conducts molten/aqueous only. NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ etc. act as ions.
Covalent: non-metals, unpaired electrons, opposite spins; σ (head-on) > π (sidewise); gas/liquid/soft; low mp; non-conductor (lone pair ≠ free electron).
Coordinate: both electrons from one atom (donor → acceptor): NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, NH₃→BF₃.
Metallic: cations in a sea of delocalised electrons; conducts; ductile/malleable (rearrangement); luster; photoelectric effect.
H-bond: H on F/O/N + lone pair of another molecule, dotted line, directional; inter → high bp; intra → no effect; ice floats (open hexagonal cage).
