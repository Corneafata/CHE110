  2. Writing questions are "creative style": under a stem there are 2 to 5 questions; answers are 2–5 lines and the answer space is tiny ("you will divide the space into three parts").
  3. Short questions examples she gave: "find the unit of the rate constant", "draw the graph of a first-order reaction: rate vs concentration", a small maths problem such as "the slope of the tangent is given: find the instantaneous rate" (divide by the coefficient; negative slope = reactant, positive = product).
  4. Maths without graph paper will appear: setting rates equal through coefficients ("rate of formation of…"), half-life and time questions, order from data, Arrhenius.
  5. Show the formula, substitute the values, then give the answer with the unit (0.25 for the unit). Give the reason in one line ("rate changes in the same proportion as concentration, so first order").
  6. Don't copy the question. For yes/no, write "Yes"/"No" and go straight to the reason. Don't write four pages; unnecessary writing loses marks.
  7. Rough work is checked and can earn 0.5; use the rough space; if you cross out, don't cross out a correct answer.
  8. Bring your own calculator, pencil, pen, scale; a calculator slip (wrong entry) loses 0.25–0.5.

Must-know facts by lecture

Lecture 1: Atomic models

  • Strong nuclear force holds protons and neutrons; Coulombic attraction holds electrons; neutron = "peacemaker"; n:p ≈ 1 stable (C-12), too many neutrons → radioactive (C-14).
  • Rutherford: nucleus, mostly empty space, solar-system analogy; fails because Maxwell says a moving charge loses energy and spirals in (Rutherford and Maxwell contradict: true).
  • Bohr: orbits; fixed energy; angular momentum quantized mvr = nh/2π (n = orbit number, not orbital); semi-classical; explains only single-electron species.
  • Energy packets: an electron absorbs only the exact gap (30 J, not 10 + 20); high → low emits ΔE = hc/λ; Rydberg 1/λ = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), n₂ higher/initial; series Lyman 1, Balmer 2, Paschen 3, Brackett 4, Pfund 5.
  • Spectrum: line vs continuous (white light continuous); emission = bright lines, absorption = dark lines. Slides: Dalton's postulates/limitations; isotopes, isobars, isotones; Thomson.

Lecture 2: Bohr follow-ups and quantum ideas

  • Max energy / min λ: from ∞; min energy / max λ: from n₁ + 1 (lower level first).
  • Radius ↑, velocity ↓ with n; c is the photon's speed; electron path elliptical (Sommerfeld).
  • de Broglie λ = h/mv (humans have λ); Heisenberg Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π; Schrödinger wave functions.
  • Nodes: radial n−l−1, angular l, total n−1; 1s none; s no angular node.

Lecture 3: Quantum numbers, configuration

  • n energy level; l shape (0…n−1); mₗ orientation (−l…+l; 0 = z); mₛ spin ±½. Invalid: 3f, 2d, 1p, mₗ out of range.
  • Pauli (4 QNs unique), Aufbau (n + l; 4s before 3d), Hund (singly first); half/full-filled stable; Cr, Cu exceptions; configurations 1–30.

Lecture 4: Periodicity

  • Z_eff = Z − σ. Down a group: shielding ↑ → radius ↑, IE ↓. Across a period: Z_eff ↑ → radius ↓, IE ↑. (Use both terms in 2–3 lines.)
  • Cation < atom, anion > atom; isoelectronic: Al³⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻; Fe⁴⁺ < Fe³⁺ < Fe²⁺.
  • IE endothermic; exceptions N > O, Be > B; noble gas highest IE/lowest EA; EA exceptions C > N, Cl > F; EN: bonded electron, no half/full effect; EA = free electron.
  • Diagonal: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si; d-block colour (d–d transition) and alloys (vacant space); defects of the table.

Lecture 5: Rate and factors

  • Rate = change in concentration per time; initial / average / instantaneous (tangent). Rate always positive; divide by the coefficient for the reaction rate.
  • Reactant curve steepest at the start (more effective collisions); six factors; catalyst lowers Eₐ via a new route; temperature raises KE (not lowers Eₐ).

Lecture 6: Rate law and order

  • Write "Rate = k[A]^x[B]^y"; reactants only; no coefficients unless elementary; overall order = sum.
  • Rate vs [A]: 0 horizontal, 1 straight line through the origin (slope = k), 2 parabola. [A] vs t: 0 straight, 1 curve, 2 curve above.
  • Zero: [A] = [A]₀ − kt, t½ = [A]₀/2k, unit M s⁻¹; first: ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt, t½ = 0.693/k, unit s⁻¹; second: 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt, t½ = 1/(k[A]₀), unit M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Order from data by ratio; % complete → use remaining.

Lecture 7: Mechanism, PE diagram, Arrhenius

  • Elementary (one step, powers = coefficients); molecularity (whole number, theoretical) vs order (experimental, 0 or fractional); high molecularity rare; RDS = slowest step (true); intermediate vs product.
  • PE diagram: Eₐ = TS − reactant; ΔH = product − reactant; exo/endo; TS/activated complex highest. Temperature doesn't lower Eₐ; collision theory = energy + orientation.
  • Arrhenius k = Ae^(−Eₐ/RT); ln k vs 1/T slope −Eₐ/R; two-T formula (K, J/mol, remove ln first).

Lecture 8: Bonding

  • Bond PE curve: zero line, minimum (attraction = repulsion), bond length, bond energy, repulsive/attractive regions; formation exothermic, breaking endothermic.
  • Ionic: metal + non-metal (NH₄⁺ acts as a metal); lattice energy ↑ with charge ↑, size ↓; solid, brittle (like charges repel), dissolves in water not CCl₄; conducts molten/aqueous only.
  • Covalent: unpaired electrons, opposite spins; no free electrons (lone pair ≠ free); σ stronger than π. Coordinate: both electrons from one atom (NH₄⁺, NH₃→BF₃). Metallic: electron sea; conducts; ductile; photoelectric effect.
  • H-bond: H on F/O/N; dotted line; intermolecular → abnormal bp (H₂O vs H₂S); intramolecular → no effect; ice floats (open hexagonal cage).

Quiz questions the lecturer reviewed (high-yield)

She went through these after the quizzes. Variants of them will return.

QuestionAnswer and the point she made
Which pair is not diagonally related (C and S?)C and S. Carbon (2,4) is diagonal to phosphorus (2,8,5): 4 valence electrons ↔ 5 in the next period; sulfur has 6, so it's not the neighbour.
Doubling the concentration makes the initial rate four times. Order?2. What power on the concentration gives 4? 2² = 4.
Unit of k for a first-order reaction?s⁻¹ (per time).
Which is a unimolecular reaction? (2X → X₂ was a wrong choice)Molecularity = number of reactant particles in the balanced elementary equation; 2X → X₂ has two particles, so it's bimolecular.
T/F: "For an elementary reaction the orders are always equal to the coefficients."True (2X → X₂: rate = k[X]²; molecularity 2). Many marked false.
T/F: "Non-metals generally have lower ionization energy" (plus a second half)False. Non-metals have high IE and high EA. "Half false means the whole statement is false": read the full line.
PE vs progress diagram: what is the peak position called?Transition state (activated complex), not activation energy. Eₐ is the height from the reactant to the peak, not from the axis.
What does zero activation energy mean?No barrier: the reaction is extremely fast (explosive or radioactive); it need not be "not happening".
T/F: "A reaction with zero activation energy is independent of temperature."True: with no barrier there's no need to raise the kinetic energy.
Quiz 1: pure compound, fixed compositionFixed ratio of atoms means fixed mass ratio (law of definite proportions). "Pure compound" ≠ mixture.
Quiz 1: can Bohr's model explain C, N, B?No: only single-electron systems (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺).
Quiz 1: maximum/minimum wavelength and energyThink energy difference first: from ∞ is largest (min λ); from the next level is smallest (max λ). "These will be there up to the final." They are photon energies.

Traps and marks lost (all lectures)

Concepts
  • n is the orbit number, not the orbital; max λ comes from the nearest level; lower level first in Rydberg.
  • Anion > atom; cation < atom; isoelectronic by protons.
  • Activation energy ≠ the peak; temperature does not lower Eₐ.
  • Lone pair ≠ free electron; solid ionic crystals don't conduct.
  • Dotted line = hydrogen bond; solid = covalent.
  • Use "intermolecular hydrogen bonding" as the term in ice/water answers.
  • Lattice energy: use charge/size, not electronegativity.
Writing and maths
  • Missing units (−0.25); wrong calculator entry; not dividing by the coefficient.
  • No "Rate =" in the rate law; product in the rate law; coefficients as powers.
  • Using % reacted instead of % remaining.
  • Moving numbers across ln before removing it; °C or kJ not converted.
  • Copying the question into the answer; writing essays; unreadable handwriting.
  • Marking "peak" or "slowest step" statements false by mistake.
  • Half-read true/false statements.
