Lecture 6: Rate law, order and integrated rate laws

Built from the transcripts "L4" and "L5" (they cover Lecture 5–6, slides 23–36) and the slides. Slides 1–22 (reaction types, rate definitions, graphs, factors) are in the Tutorial guide. This page is Lecture 6 (slides 23–36 of the Lecture 5–6 deck). TAUGHT IN CLASS EXAM SIGNAL SLIDES

Midterm: the syllabus runs through Lecture 8, so all of this page is in scope. Format (20 marks, 1 hour) and strategy: Midterm prep.

What the lecturer said about the exam

  1. "Rate law and the graphs of the orders are definitely in the final. There is no scope to skip them." They come as 1–1.5 mark short questions, MCQs, and as sub-parts of broad questions.
  2. Write "Rate = …" (a law is a relationship). Writing only the right-hand side loses half the marks.
  3. Only reactants appear in the rate law, never products.
  4. Never use the coefficients as powers unless the question says the reaction is elementary or gives the order. Otherwise use unknowns x, y, z.
  5. Graphs: "rate vs concentration" for zero, first and second order, sometimes all three on one graph; and "concentration vs time". They can be tentative graphs without scale. Intersections of the three curves don't matter; "as long as your trends are right".
  6. True/false signals: "In a first-order reaction the slope of rate vs concentration is the rate constant." (True)
  7. Know the unit of k for zero, first and second order. You may be asked "what is the order?" just from the unit (1 mark).
  8. Derivations are not asked but know the equations: if you can write the rate law you can derive it in two minutes. "Many people don't remember so many formulas, so derive."
  9. Maths questions are small (3–4 minutes each): find k, find time, find half-life, find concentration after t, find order from data. "From what I taught today you will definitely get one broad question (about 5 marks) in the midterm."
  10. Calculator, units and extra writing cost marks: a wrong calculator entry costs 0.25–0.5; a missing unit costs 0.25; not dividing by the coefficient cost 0.25; unnecessary explanations are penalised. No unit conversion (min → s, etc.) is ever asked.
  11. The lecturer said a second tutorial will include these order questions (MCQ and short; "even without maths, understand them by looking"). Date not stated in the recordings.
  12. The midterm broad questions are "creative style" (a stem with 3–4 parts), short answers of 2–5 lines, tiny answer space.

1 · Writing the rate law

CLASSSLIDE 23EXAM SIGNAL
  • The reaction rate is proportional to each reactant concentration raised to some power. Found by varying one reactant concentration while keeping the other constant.
rate = k [A]m [B]n
  • A law is a relationship: here between the rate and the concentration of the reactants. k = rate constant (replaces the proportionality sign).
  • The powers (m, n) are the orders with respect to A and B. We cannot know them without an experiment, so use unknown variables (x, y, z, a, b, c, in small letters). Different letters for different reactants ("sometimes the order can be the same too").
Reaction (order not given)Rate law
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂Rate = k[CaCO₃]x
H₂ + I₂ → 2HIRate = k[H₂]x[I₂]y
2NO + 2H₂ → N₂ + 2H₂ORate = k[NO]x[H₂]y
A → B, zero / first / second order givenRate = k[A]⁰ = k; Rate = k[A]¹; Rate = k[A]²
Mistakes the lecturer sees every semester
  1. Writing only "k[A]…" without Rate = (loses half the marks).
  2. Including the product concentration. Only reactants.
  3. Using the coefficients as powers (e.g. putting [H₂]² because there is a 2). Don't, unless told it is elementary or the order is given.
Elementary reactions (single step, one collision). Only for these can the order be read from the coefficients. Example: H₂ + I₂ → 2HI (one H₂ collides with one I₂), so Rate = k[H₂]¹[I₂]¹, overall order 2. The question will say "elementary"; you are not expected to memorise which reactions are elementary. Multi-step (composite) reactions can have intermediates, so the order can't be read off. (More in the Lecture 7 guide.)

Why only reactants? We look at the forward reaction and how the reactant concentration changes the rate (the reaction is usually stopped after some time). If the reactant concentration keeps increasing, the rate increases. Each reactant changes the rate differently, hence individual powers.

Don't mix up two things (tutorial class). "The rate of disappearance of A" = |d[A]/dt| only (no coefficient). "The rate of the reaction" expressed through A = |d[A]/dt| ÷ (coefficient of A). For 2A → 3B: ½|d[A]/dt| = ⅓ d[B]/dt. Rates are always positive (use the modulus for a reactant).

Average, instantaneous or initial rate: the "rate" in the law can be any of the three. Averages and instantaneous rates are found from a given reactant/product concentration vs time (tutorial work); the order tells how the rate depends on concentration.

2 · Order of a reaction

SLIDE 24CLASS
  • Order = the power to which a concentration is raised in the rate law. Overall order = the sum of the powers (x + y). If x = 2 and y = 1 the reaction is third order overall. Order is experimentally determined.
  • Possible values: 0, 1, 2, 3, 3/2, 1/2. 3/2 and 1/2 occur for gas-phase reactions only; third order is rare; most reactions are first order; zero and third order (A + A + A → product) are rare.
  • Slide example: 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F, rate = k[NO₂][F₂] (found by experiment). First order in NO₂, first order in F₂, overall order = 2. The coefficient 2 on NO₂ does not become a power.
  • Don't memorise the orders shown on slides; "they did experiments to get them."
  • Orders can differ if conditions (temperature) change.

4 · Zero-order reactions

SLIDE 26CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
  • The rate does not depend on the concentration of the reactant: Rate = k[A]⁰ = k. Real examples are very rare (a few cases only).
  • Derivation (not asked, but good to know): rate = −d[A]/dt = k → d[A] = −k dt → integrate from [A]₀ (t = 0) to [A] (t):
    [A] = [A]₀ − kt
    (the lecturer's approach: write the rate law, integrate; "doesn't take two minutes").
  • Half-life ([A] = [A]₀/2):
    t½ = [A]₀ / 2k
  • Unit of k = unit of rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ = M s⁻¹): the rate and k have the same unit only for zero order. In data tables, if the rate does not change when concentration changes, it is zero order and k = the rate.
  • [A] vs t: a straight line, slope = −k. Because it is a straight line, average rate = instantaneous rate = initial rate (any two points give the same slope).
  • Slide problem. [A]₀ = 1.5 M, [A] = 0.75 M after 120 s. Find k: k = ([A]₀ − [A])/t = 0.75/120 = 6.25×10⁻³ M s⁻¹. (The lecturer also warned: "in the calculator it shows 0.556 and some wrote 0.005 or 25.something": copy values carefully. Estimate first: e.g. 20/5 = 4.)

5 · First-order reactions

SLIDES 27–28CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
  • Rate = k[A] (depends linearly on one reactant). For A → products: at t = 0, [A] = [A]₀ (or a); after time t, [A] = a − x.
  • Derivation: −d[A]/dt = k[A] → d[A]/[A] = −k dt → ∫ from [A]₀ to [A] gives ln[A]₀ − ln[A] = kt:
    ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt  ⇔  [A] = [A]₀ e−kt  ⇔  ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt
    Same form as radioactive decay N = N₀e^(−λt). Use whichever form you like; the ratio form is easiest because [A]₀ cancels in percentage problems.
  • Half-life ([A] = [A]₀/2): ln 2 = kt½, so
    t½ = ln 2 / k = 0.693 / k
    No concentration term, so the half-life is independent of the initial concentration (a way to recognise first order).
  • Unit of k: time⁻¹ (s⁻¹, min⁻¹). The concentration units cancel (M / M). Seeing per-second tells you "first order".
  • Log version (slide 27 graphs): log₁₀ form: log([A]₀/[A]) = kt/2.303. The slide plots log(a/(a−x)) vs t (labelled "slope = k"; strictly the slope is k/2.303 for log₁₀) and log(a − x) vs t (slope −k/2.303). With natural logs the slopes are exactly k and −k.
  • Radioactive decay is first order. One particle breaks down on its own (the nucleus), in one step: no collisions, so temperature and concentration don't affect it. Used in carbon dating (age determination). You don't need to find the order for radioactive materials; say "first order". Only the form differs: particle numbers (via Avogadro's number) vs concentration.

Slide problems (worked)

Slide 27: "35.5% complete in 4.90 min. Find k."
Use the remaining amount: 100 − 35.5 = 64.5%. (Don't plug the percentage used.) No need for [A]₀: ratio [A]₀/[A] = 100/64.5. k = (1/4.90) ln(100/64.5) = 0.4385/4.90 = 0.0895 min⁻¹. Why remaining? Order is measured from what is left in the beaker, which is easy to measure.

Slide 28: "k = 0.450 s⁻¹. Half-life?" t½ = 0.693/0.450 = 1.54 s (no concentration needed).

Slide 28: "50% complete in 23 min. Time for 90%?" "50% complete" means one half-life: t½ = 23 min, so k = 0.693/23 = 0.0301 min⁻¹ (note the unit). For 90% complete, 10% remains: t = (1/k) ln(100/10) = 2.303/0.0301 = 76.4 min. Whether you put [A]₀ = 100 and [A] = 10, or [A]₀ and 0.10[A]₀, it cancels. Write the unit.

6 · Second-order reactions

SLIDES 29–30CLASS (read on your own)
  • Rate depends on [A]² (or on two different reactants, each to the first power). For 2A → products: Rate = k[A]².
  • From the slide summary table:
    1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt    t½ = 1 / (k[A]₀)
  • Unit of k: L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ (M⁻¹ s⁻¹).
  • [A] vs t: a curve like first order, but "the change in concentration is a bit greater", so it lies slightly above the first-order curve.
  • Slide problems. k = 0.025 M⁻¹s⁻¹, [A]₀ = 0.80 M, t = 200 s: 1/[A] = 1/0.80 + 0.025 × 200 = 1.25 + 5 = 6.25, so [A] = 0.16 M. k = 0.010 M⁻¹s⁻¹, [A]₀ = 0.50 M: t½ = 1/(0.010 × 0.50) = 200 s.

8 · Finding the order from data

SLIDES 33–35CLASSEXAM SIGNAL
Why initial rates are used (tutorial class). The initial rate (at t = 0) is used to find the order of reactions with several reactants or steps. As the reaction proceeds, each reactant changes differently and product accumulates, so you can't tell which concentration change caused which change in rate. At the start only your chosen change matters. "Most order problems are done with the initial rate."

The method

  1. Write the rate law first (marks are for it): Rate = k[NO]^x[H₂]^y. Unknowns: k, x, y.
  2. Ratio of two experiments so that k cancels.
  3. With two reactants, hold one concentration fixed, choose experiments where it does not change, and find the other's order. Then swap.
  4. Overall order = x + y. Find k from any one experiment (you now know the powers), and find its unit.
Slide 33: 2NO + 2H₂ → N₂ + 2H₂O
Exp[NO] (M)[H₂] (M)Initial rate (M s⁻¹)

Rate law: Rate = k[NO]^x[H₂]^y.
Find x: [H₂] fixed in experiments 1 and 3. Rate₁/Rate₃ = 2.4/9.6 = 1/4 = (0.10/0.20)^x = (1/2)^x → x = 2.
Find y: [NO] fixed in experiments 1 and 2. Rate₁/Rate₂ = 2.4/1.2 = 2 = (0.20/0.10)^y → y = 1.
Rate = k[NO]²[H₂]; overall order = 3. k = 2.4×10⁻³/(0.10² × 0.20) = 1.2 M⁻² s⁻¹.
Unit of k: overall order 3 → concentration appears three times; rate (M s⁻¹) ÷ M³ = M⁻² s⁻¹ (carries half a mark).

Slide 34: SO₂Cl₂ → SO₂ + Cl₂: rate = 3.60×10⁻⁶ M s⁻¹ at [SO₂Cl₂] = 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 M.
Rate law: Rate = k[SO₂Cl₂]^x. The rate does not change with concentration → zero order (write the one-line reason). Rate = k, so k = 3.60×10⁻⁶ M s⁻¹. Graphs: rate vs [A] is a horizontal line; [A] vs t is a straight line sloping down.
Slide 35: decomposition of O₃: [O₃] = 0.020, 0.040, 0.060 M; rate = 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 ×10⁻⁵ M s⁻¹.
Rate law: Rate = k[O₃]^x. Concentration ratios 1/2 and 2/3 give identical rate ratios 1/2 and 2/3 → first order. (You may state "rate increases in the same proportion as concentration".) k = rate/[O₃] = 1.0×10⁻⁵/0.020 = 5.0×10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. Unit: M s⁻¹ ÷ M = s⁻¹.
The lecturer cautioned: "Does decomposition always happen in a single step? Most don't." The order here comes from the table, not from the reaction type.

9 · Exam technique (from the transcripts)

  • For any maths problem: (1) write the rate law, (2) identify the order (stated, from the unit of k, or from data), (3) write the equation for that order, (4) substitute, (5) write the answer with a unit.
  • Order from the unit of k: s⁻¹ → 1; M s⁻¹ → 0; M⁻¹ s⁻¹ → 2.
  • If the order is stated but you forget the equation, derive it (write the rate law as −d[A]/dt and integrate).
  • Substitute remaining amounts for percentage problems. [A]₀ cancels.
  • When k is given and you need [A] after t: use [A] = [A]₀e^(−kt) for first order or the relevant form. When solving ln(x) = −2 you must first remove the ln (use e^(−2)); don't move numbers across before that.
  • Calculator: bring your own. Estimate the answer first (20/5 is about 4). Several students entered correct values but copied 0.556 as 0.005. Take care.
  • No unit conversion. Write the unit that comes out of k (e.g. minutes).
  • Short graph questions: label axes, show trends, no scale needed.
  • Writing: be brief and legible. For "why zero order" write one line: "rate does not change with concentration".
