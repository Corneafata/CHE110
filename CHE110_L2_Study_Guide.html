1 · Bohr's model: follow-ups

TAUGHT IN CLASS

Energy at infinity, and the big energy gap

  • At n = ∞ the electron is almost free: it feels no attraction from the nucleus.
  • For an electron falling from ∞ to a low level (1 or 2), the energy difference is the maximum, because you are subtracting from a level where it is essentially free.
  • Close to the nucleus the attraction is strong; the first orbit's electron is the most strongly bound, so the gap to the free state is the biggest.
  • Easier to remove an electron from a higher level. "We do not need to provide as much energy to remove an electron from a higher level, because the higher you go, the attraction decreases."
Two ways the word "energy" was used (read the question carefully).
• Lecture 1: the energy of the level increases with n (E₁ < E₂ < … , n = ∞ highest; in numbers E = −13.6 eV/n², rising from −13.6 toward 0).
• Lecture 2 (opening, answering a student): the lecturer said the first-orbit electron has the "highest" energy and that energy "decreases" as you go higher. Here she was talking about the binding energy (how strongly the electron is held, the energy needed to free it), which is largest at n = 1 and falls with n.
Both are consistent. Level energy rises with n; binding / ionization energy falls with n. If a question says "energy needed to remove / ionization / attraction", use the falling version. If it says "energy of the orbit", use the rising version. If a question is ambiguous, ask the lecturer which convention she wants.

Radius and velocity

  • As you move farther from the nucleus, the radius increases and the velocity decreases. EXAM SIGNAL
  • This comes from the limitations of Bohr's model.

Whose velocity is "c"?

  • c is the velocity of a photon, not the electron. A photon is massless, so it travels at the speed of light.
  • A photon comes in and excites an electron. When the electron drops back down it releases the same photon (same energy) it absorbed.
  • An electron's velocity is never c; it is always less than the speed of light.

Circular or elliptical? (Sommerfeld)

  • Bohr said the electron moves in a circular path, so the radius is constant and the velocity is fixed.
  • In reality the electron moves in an elliptical path. In the same orbit it is sometimes very close to the nucleus (velocity higher) and sometimes far (velocity lower).
  • Sommerfeld later explained this and corrected many aspects of Bohr's model (the Sommerfeld model).

Is angular momentum still quantized? (the 5 × 10 = 10 × 5 idea)

In an elliptical orbit r changes and v changes. If r gets smaller, v gets larger, and the product stays the same (like 5 × 10 = 10 × 5 = 50). Mass is constant. So mvr stays fixed, which proves the angular-momentum quantization is correct. But Bohr only gave the formula. His circular-path picture is wrong, and he gave no logical explanation. Scientists saw that the formula worked but wouldn't fully accept it without an explanation. de Broglie supplied that explanation (section 3).

2 · The series: maximum and minimum wavelength

TAUGHT IN CLASSCOMMON EXAM QUESTION

Spectrum recap

  • An electron absorbs a photon's energy → absorption (moves up). When it drops from a higher level to a lower one it emits the photon → emission (moves down).
  • The radiation we usually observe belongs to emission spectra. All five series (Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund) are emission (high → low), each with a fixed lower limit.
  • If the series name is given, you should immediately know n₁.
Series order and n₁: Lyman 1 · Balmer 2 · Paschen 3 · Brackett 4 · Pfund 5.

The rules

CaseTransitionEnergyWavelength
Maximum energyFrom n = ∞ down to n₁MaxMinimum λ
Minimum energyFrom the immediate next level (n₁ + 1) to n₁MinMaximum λ

Energy and wavelength are inversely proportional, so learn the transition by either energy or wavelength and derive the other.

Common mistake (lecturer): even after the series is given, students put the higher value in n₁. The lower level goes first. A series means the scientist fixed a lower limit; the electron can come from any higher level, from the immediate one all the way up to infinity.

3 · de Broglie: dual nature

TAUGHT IN CLASSTRUE / FALSE SIGNAL
  • de Broglie proposed that electrons (and other particles) have a dual nature: particle (they have mass) and wave. Light was already known to be dual.
  • He logically supported Bohr's formula using this idea (the wave explanation of quantized orbits).
λ = h / (m v)
  • mv is ordinary momentum (mass × velocity), not angular momentum.
  • High velocity → high frequency → very small wavelength. Low velocity → large wavelength.
  • Everything has a wavelength. A 50 kg person running at 100 m/s: λ ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (50 × 100) ≈ 10⁻³⁷ m. This is negligible (can't be measured or seen), but not non-existent. A grain of sand and a multi-ton elephant also have one.
  • The wavelength of electrons, protons and neutrons is measurable, because their mass is tiny. Example: electron m ≈ 10⁻³¹ kg, v ≈ 2.8×10⁸ m/s → λ ≈ 10⁻¹² m (10⁻³⁴ in the numerator over 10⁻³¹ in the denominator gives a difference of 10³).
True/false trap: "According to de Broglie, human beings do not have a wavelength." → FALSE. We have one; it's negligible. Nobody said it doesn't exist.
de Broglie calculator
Use λ = h/mv with h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s.

The anecdote

de Broglie's PhD thesis was one page: electrons, like light, show both particle and wave nature. The board couldn't decide and asked Einstein, who replied that the student knew more than they did. The PhD was accepted. (Told to show the idea is simple but wasn't easy to arrive at.)

4 · Heisenberg and Schrödinger

TAUGHT IN CLASS

Does the electron show both natures at once?

No. It shows one extreme at a time, and the other is suppressed (like a person showing only their good side at a time). This uncertainty is what Heisenberg explained.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle

Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4π
  • x = position, p = momentum (mass × velocity).
  • Think of the "=" version: if one is very large, the other must be small.
  • If the position is fixed (certain), you cannot determine the velocity. If you measure the velocity accurately, you cannot fix the position. When one becomes certain, the other becomes uncertain.
  • Mass is treated as constant (relativistic mass change is not applied here).
Analogy used: a moving object. If you can pinpoint its exact location, its velocity must be low (it is nearly still); if it is moving fast you can't pinpoint where it is.

Schrödinger and quantum mechanics

  • The modern theory is quantum mechanics, based on Schrödinger's wave equation.
  • Instead of saying an electron jumps from "orbit ∞ to orbit 2", we say it moves from one wave-function (ψ) state to another. The word orbit is no longer needed; you use wave functions and mathematical equations.
  • The equation contains mass, Planck's constant, total energy and potential energy. You only need to know that it exists; do not memorise it. Solving it takes calculus (differentiation, integration).
  • Solving it gives values: the probability of finding the electron at a location, its position, direction and how it moves along an axis. These values are the quantum numbers (n, l, mₗ, mₛ).
  • For a single electron (e.g. H) it can be solved. With many electrons it can no longer be solved easily.
  • Slide: ψ² gives the probability of finding the particle in a region of space. SLIDES
  • The combination of de Broglie, Heisenberg and Schrödinger explains an electron's nature and gives the modern atomic model.

5 · Orbitals, electron clouds and nodes

TAUGHT IN CLASSMCQ / SHORT-QUESTION SIGNAL

Orbit vs orbital (analogy)

  • An orbit is like a building (or road); an orbital is like the specific flat or room in it.
  • Electrons exist in orbitals, never in the spaces between them. Most of the atom is empty space (Rutherford), which is why atomic radii are large.
  • Electrons are not on thin lines. An electron exists as a cloud; the next level has its own cloud.

What is a node?

The thin regions between clouds are like edges of two waves meeting with destructive interference. Electrons are found where waves match, not where they cancel. A node is a region where the probability of finding an electron is (almost) zero. You will never find an electron there.

Two types of node

Radial nodeAngular node
MeaningA spherical region at a particular distance (radius) from the nucleus where probability = 0 (e.g. the white gap inside a 2s picture)A plane through the nucleus where probability = 0 (between the positive and negative lobes of a p orbital)
Formulan − l − 1l
Totaln − 1 (radial + angular)
  • s orbital: spherical, equal in x, y, z. There is no phase change, so no angular node (l = 0). It can only have radial nodes.
  • p, d, f have lobes with positive and negative phase, so they have angular nodes (= l).
  • 1s has no node at all: it is the closest to the nucleus, and there's nothing between it and the nucleus. n − l − 1 = 1 − 0 − 1 = 0; l = 0. Nodes only appear at higher levels.
  • 2s: 1 radial node, 0 angular. 3s: 2 radial. 3p (the picture): 1 radial + 1 angular (the line through the middle) = 2 total.
  • The nodes increase as you go further, and the cloud density spreads out.

Radial probability curves (to understand, not to draw)

The graph has distance on the x-axis and probability on the y-axis. 1s: never touches zero (no node). 2s: touches zero once (1 node). 3s: touches zero twice (2 nodes). Where the curve hits zero is a node at that radius. The lecturer said you are not asked to draw these.

Aside (1s and electron capture). 1s electrons are so close to the nucleus that the nucleus can sometimes capture one (electron capture). An electron from a higher level then drops into the vacancy and releases energy (the same idea as X-rays). Mentioned in passing; not an exam point.

6 · The four quantum numbers

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL

Quantum numbers come from solving the wave equation. Together they give each electron a unique identity.

SymbolNameWhat it indicatesAllowed valuesAnalogy
nPrincipalEnergy level (shell); K, L, M, N… for 1, 2, 3, 4…1, 2, 3, …The building (house number)
lAzimuthal / subsidiaryOrbital name / shape (s, p, d, f)0 to n − 1 (0 = s, 1 = p, 2 = d, 3 = f)The flat and its shape
mₗMagneticOrientation (direction) in 3D space−l to +l (2l + 1 values), integers onlyWhich room, in which direction
mₛSpinSpin of the electron on its own axis (clockwise / counter-clockwise)+½ or −½How you are positioned inside the room
  • Numbers can be written as symbols too: n as K, L, M, N; l as s, p, d, f (easier to see the shape).
  • l → shape: s spherical, p dumbbell, d double dumbbell.
  • mₛ: in one orbital (one direction), at most two electrons: one spins one way, the other the opposite way. The ± sign is the direction relative to a reference. (½ is explained as one possibility out of two: clockwise or counter-clockwise. You don't need the deeper physics.)
  • The ± sign of mₗ is the direction relative to the z-axis. z is the reference, so mₗ = 0 is the z orbital.
Short-question tip: "Which quantum number indicates orientation in 3D space?" → magnetic (mₗ). Write exactly what is asked; no explanation needed.

Possible values for n = 3 (class example)

  • l = 0 to n − 1 = 0, 1, 2 → s, p, d (3 values of l).
  • mₗ for s: 0 → 1 value. For p: −1, 0, +1 → 3. For d: −2 … +2 → 5. Total mₗ values = 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = n² (shortcut).
  • Max electrons in the shell = 2n² = 18.
  • The lecturer: if unsure of a shortcut, do it step by step.

Are these four quantum numbers valid? (true/false)

n = 4 → l can be 0, 1, 2, 3 → l = 2 is allowed. But l = 2 → mₗ must be between −2 and +2 → +3 is not allowed. So the set is invalid, and the statement "this set is valid" is false. Check them one by one: n, then l, then mₗ, then mₛ.

7 · Electron configuration

TAUGHT IN CLASSEXAM SIGNAL: atoms 1 to 30

The electron configuration tells you where each of the electrons sits. The 118 elements are explained with the four orbital types s, p, d, f (g and h are not known to exist yet). Example: sodium, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ (11 electrons): first fill s, then p, then 3s in the next shell. It is not random.

Aufbau principle ("build-up")

  • Electrons fill from low energy to high energy. The energy order of subshells is s < p < d < f. s is lowest because s electrons stay close to the nucleus: higher attraction and penetration, so electrons enter s first.
  • (n + l) rule: the orbital with the lower n + l fills first. If n + l is equal, the one with the lower n fills first.
  • Example: 4s (4 + 0 = 4) fills before 3d (3 + 2 = 5), even though 3d is in a lower shell.
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s …

Pauli exclusion principle

  • No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. The quantum numbers are each electron's unique identity (like names for twins).
  • Two electrons in the same orbital (e.g. 2p_x): n, l, mₗ are the same, so mₛ must differ (+½ and −½). Hence an orbital holds maximum two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • If they were identical, they would repel and the atom wouldn't exist. Nature stays balanced.

Hund's rule

  • Degenerate orbitals = orbitals of the same energy: p has 3, d has 5, f has 7.
  • Electrons enter degenerate orbitals one by one first, all with the same spin. Pairing starts only after every orbital has one electron (siblings and chocolates analogy: give one to each so nobody fights).
  • Reason: a half-filled set is a symmetric structure with less repulsion. Half-filled and fully-filled sets are stable.
  • The first electron may go into any of the degenerate orbitals (p_x, p_y or p_z); there's no rule about which first.
  • Examples (slides): N (7) = 1s² 2s² 2p_x¹ 2p_y¹ 2p_z¹. O (8) = 1s² 2s² 2p_x² 2p_y¹ 2p_z¹ (the fourth p electron pairs up in p_x with opposite spin).

Exceptions: half- and full-filled stability

If 4s² 3d⁹ were the configuration, moving one electron from 4s into 3d gives 3d¹⁰ (full), which is more stable. So copper (29) is 4s¹ 3d¹⁰, not 4s² 3d⁹. Likewise chromium (24) is 4s¹ 3d⁵ (half-filled d) instead of 4s² 3d⁴. The lecturer's rule: "half-filled and full-filled are stable". Atoms 1–30 contain these exceptions.

Slides extras (Lecture 2–3)

SLIDES Points on the slides that the lecturer didn't spend time on in the audio. Quiz-ready, but lower priority than the class notes above.
  • Orbit: electrons move in two-dimensional, spherical, fixed energy levels called orbits; K (nearest the nucleus), L, M, N…
  • Orbital: the 3D region where the probability of finding the electron is maximum (90–95%). s, p, d, f; max two electrons per orbital. The idea of orbitals came from the wave nature of electrons (not part of Bohr's theory). Atomic orbital model = electron cloud / wave mechanics model.
  • Orbit vs orbital: orbit is a fixed circular path (Bohr, 2D, particle picture). Orbital is a 3D probability region (wave picture).
  • Shapes: s spherical; p dumbbell (three: pₓ, p_y, p_z, same energy, along perpendicular axes); d: five orbitals; f: seven orbitals.
  • Balmer proposed the formula for the wave number of spectral lines in 1885; the Rydberg formula relates the energy difference between Bohr levels to the wavelengths of absorbed/emitted photons.
  • Spectrum: Newton showed sunlight is made of colours. An incandescent bulb gives a continuous spectrum (all wavelengths). Emission: heated atoms emit certain wavelengths (bright lines). Absorption: white light through a substance leaves dark lines.
  • Bohr slide statements: the first model with quantum energy levels; won the Nobel Prize in 1922; closer orbits (lower energy) are more stable; an electron in the lowest energy orbit is in the ground state; absorbing energy to a higher level gives an excited state.
  • Bohr limitations (slides): explains only single-electron species; cannot explain fine structure (a line splits into several close lines → sub-levels → new quantum numbers); cannot explain splitting in magnetic or electric fields; the idea of electrons jumping between orbits conflicts with the uncertainty in position and velocity; circular vs elliptical.
  • Link: 3D images of nodes at chemtube3d.com (3p orbitals).

Worked problems

1. Radial, angular and total nodes in 4d. n = 4, l = 2. Radial = 4 − 2 − 1 = 1; angular = 2; total = 3.
2. Nodes in 5f. n = 5, l = 3. Radial = 5 − 3 − 1 = 1; angular = 3; total = 4.
3. Nodes in 1s. Radial = 1 − 0 − 1 = 0; angular = 0. No node.
4. n = 3: possible l, mₗ. l = 0, 1, 2; mₗ count = 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 (= n²).
5. Orbital from n = 4, l = 2, mₗ = 0. 4d_z².
6. Is (n = 3, l = 3, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½) valid? No: l must be 0 to n − 1 = 2.
7. Is (n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = −1, mₛ = −½) valid? Yes: l ≤ 1; mₗ between −1 and +1; mₛ = ±½. It is a 2p electron.
8. de Broglie, 50 kg at 100 m/s. λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (50 × 100) = 1.3×10⁻³⁷ m. Negligible, not zero.
9. de Broglie, electron. m = 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg, v = 2.8×10⁸ m/s: λ = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (9.109×10⁻³¹ × 2.8×10⁸) = 2.6×10⁻¹² m (measurable).
10. Configuration of sodium (11). 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹. Last electron: n = 3, l = 0, mₗ = 0, mₛ = +½.
11. Why does 4s fill before 3d? n + l: 4s = 4 + 0 = 4; 3d = 3 + 2 = 5. Lower n + l first.
12. Oxygen (8) by Hund. 1s² 2s² 2p⁴: the first three p electrons go singly into p_x, p_y, p_z (same spin); the fourth pairs with opposite spin in one of them.
13. Series check. Balmer min λ: ∞ → 2: 4/R = 3.65×10⁻⁷ m. Lyman max λ: 2 → 1: 4/(3R) = 1.215×10⁻⁷ m.
